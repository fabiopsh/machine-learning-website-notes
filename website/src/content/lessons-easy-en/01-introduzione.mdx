## What Machine Learning is

### Learning as a universal principle

Learning is something everybody does: animals, people, groups of people and, today, machines too. Two scholars, Poggio and Shelton (*AI Magazine*, 1999), say that *the problem of learning is arguably at the very core of the problem of intelligence, both biological and artificial*. Put simply: if we understand **how learning happens**, we understand an important piece of **what it means to be intelligent**.

<Callout type="definition" title="Machine Learning">

**Machine Learning** is a part of computer science, made of both theory and practical applications. It brings together two goals:

- that of artificial intelligence, namely building **computers that are able to learn**;
- having **powerful adaptive and statistical tools** (that is, tools that adapt to the data and that use statistics), built on rigorous foundations.

In practice: Machine Learning is when a system **learns by itself**, starting from **experience** (a set of examples), to solve a task.

</Callout>

<Deep title="The three keywords of the definition" kind="intuizione">

The definition has three ingredients. We will meet them again in every lesson, so it is worth fixing them right away:

- a **system** that learns: we will call it the *model*;
- the **experience**, that is, a set of examples: we will call them the *data*;
- a **task** to be solved.

The important idea is this: **we do not write the solution ourselves**. We write a system that is able to find it on its own, by looking at the examples. It is like with a child: you do not explain the rule for recognizing a cat, you show many cats until the child learns.

</Deep>

### Why make machines learn: luxury or necessity?

ML is not a fashion: it is a **necessity**. There are two main reasons.

1. **We have more and more data** and we need to analyze them. Science itself is changing the way it works: more and more often knowledge is obtained directly from the collected data (this way of working is called *data-driven*). ML is the central tool of this change.
2. **It is hard to write by hand a program that is “intelligent” and able to adapt.** Alan Turing had already understood this: for many tasks, writing all the rules one by one is impossible. The only way to get systems that behave intelligently is to make them **learn**.

Three things together have opened a new era of artificial intelligence: **large amounts of data**, **very powerful computers** (HPC, that is, high-performance computing, and GPUs, the graphics cards used to do many calculations at once) and **flexible ML models**, that is, models able to adapt to different problems.

### When ML is needed

ML is the right choice when two conditions hold together:

- we have **little or no prior knowledge** about the problem, that is, we do not know how to write the rules of the solution;
- it is, however, fairly **easy to obtain experience**, that is, data for which we already know the correct result.

Classic examples: deciding whether an email is spam (unwanted mail), recognizing written characters, faces, speech.

<Callout type="example" title="Handwritten digit recognition">

Each digit is a small image of $8 \times 8$ pixels (a pixel is one little colored square of the image: here there are 8 rows by 8 columns, so 64 little squares). Writing by hand the rules that tell a “3” from an “8” for every possible way of writing is, in practice, impossible. So we proceed like this: we collect a great many examples of **already labeled** digits (for each one somebody has already written “this is a 3”, “this is an 8”…) and we let the system **learn a classifier** $f$.

What is $f$? It is a [function](#/prerequisiti/funzioni): a “little machine” that receives something as input and gives something back as output. Here it receives an image and gives back the correct **class**, that is, one of the ten digits ($0, 1, \dots, 9$).

</Callout>

### Application areas

Today ML is found everywhere in real systems: in OCR (the programs that read text from an image), in the interfaces between people and machines, in search engines. It is the center of an area that touches many disciplines and that, since the 1980s, has included:

- **Pattern Recognition** (recognizing regularities in the data, for example faces and speech), **Computer Vision** (making computers “see”), **Natural Language Processing** (making computers work with human language: from text classification to speech processing, up to generative AI, the kind that produces text and images);
- **Robotics**, adaptive systems and filters, intelligent sensor networks, personalized components;
- **Knowledge Discovery and Data Mining** (discovering knowledge by “digging” into large amounts of data), Information Retrieval (finding information, as a search engine does), analysis of complex data (medicine, biology, chemistry, web, marketing), financial forecasting.

In some fields — NLP, Pattern Recognition, Computer Vision — the effect has been revolutionary, to the point that people speak of a “new AI”.

Anything powerful can become dangerous if misused. But the final goal of AI and ML is to **bring benefits to people** by solving big and small problems, to **accelerate human progress** and to give “intelligence” to every other field of science: to augment our abilities, enhancing our humanity (P. ## Why study Machine Learning

Within the Master’s degree programme, ML serves to:

- know the **basic principles of learning processes**, seen from the side of computation (that is: how, concretely, a computer manages to learn);
- know **new computing paradigms** (that is, new ways of making a computer compute), such as **neural networks**. They have been studied since the 1940s and take inspiration from the brain. Today they are a set of powerful models for **function approximation** (that is, for building a function that “resembles” as closely as possible the right one, which we do not know) and they are able to make predictions. They have a solid theory behind them (*learning theory*) and they are the basis of **Deep Learning**;
- be able to **apply these models rigorously**, that is, in the correct way;
- have the foundations for all the subsequent AI courses.

### Modern ML

ML used to be a **collection of heuristic methods**: many “tricks” that worked in practice, taken from biology, statistics, neural networks, fuzzy logic… Today, instead, **general conceptual frameworks** are built, that is, a few underlying ideas that explain all those methods together. We have understood better the principles that govern learning, and this has three important consequences:

- writing different models in different mathematical or computer science “languages” **does not change the principles**;
- using ML in different fields **does not change the principles**;
- new technologies **do not change the principles**.

<Callout type="tip" title="The common thread of the course">

The real “Machine Learning” is the **core of principles and methods** needed to build, starting from data, flexible and powerful models. Individual models change over time, the principles remain: those are what is really worth learning.

</Callout>

### Three points of view on ML

<Cards>
  <Card kicker="1" title="AI methodology">A way to build **adaptive and intelligent systems**, that is, systems that adapt and behave intelligently.</Card>
  <Card kicker="2" title="Statistical learning">Deriving hypotheses from data following mathematical principles, to build powerful **predictive systems** (systems that make predictions) for intelligent data analysis.</Card>
  <Card kicker="3" title="Computer science method for innovative areas">Using the models as **tools** to tackle complex problems that touch several disciplines.</Card>
</Cards>

The course considers all three, with a **critical approach**: for each method we discuss where it works, where it does not and when it is right to use it, always looking at the underlying principles. There are two practical goals: learning to **develop new ML models** and learning to **apply the best methods available today** (the “state of the art”) to problems from other fields.

No course is strictly required: we start from scratch. If you are missing any of these, the [prerequisites](#/prerequisiti) page explains them from the beginning.

The course presents the principles of the main ways of making a machine learn from data — models and algorithms, with particular attention to neural networks — and teaches how to look at them critically. The concepts come a little at a time, from the simplest methods up to the most advanced ones, all within the same underlying idea of modern ML: the **learning of functions from examples**. The focus is on:

- the critical analysis of the characteristics that matter when designing and using ML;
- **rigorous experimental evaluation**, that is, measuring properly how well a model works: there is a big difference between *using* ML tools and using them *correctly*;
- the computational aspects of systems that learn.

### Structure of the course

The course has a precise structure: it starts from simple models and arrives at the most advanced ones, and the fundamental concepts are presented several times, through different models. The practical advice is twofold: (1) study the individual methods, (2) look for the **connections and comparisons** among them, to understand the common ideas. The common thread is understanding the relationship between two things: **model flexibility** (how much the model manages to adapt to the data) and **complexity control** (not letting it become too complicated). The capacity of <T id="generalizzazione">generalization</T>, that is, the ability to answer well also on data never seen before, depends on this balance.

Before starting, a rule about **how things are written** (the “notation”), which holds for the whole course:

- $x$, a normal lowercase letter: a **scalar**, that is, a single number (for example $3$ or $-0.5$);
- $\mathbf{x}$, a lowercase letter in bold: a **vector**, that is, an ordered list of numbers (for example $(3, 1)$);
- $X$, an uppercase letter: a **matrix**, that is, a table of numbers with rows and columns.

### Dot product and norms

The dot product is an operation that takes **two vectors** (two lists of numbers of the same length) and gives back **a single number**. It is computed like this: multiply the numbers that sit in the same position and then add up all the results.

<Callout type="definition" title="Dot product (inner / dot product)">

<Formula f={F.dot} />

**In plain words.** The first piece, $a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + \dots + a_n b_n$, says: first number of $\mathbf{a}$ times first number of $\mathbf{b}$, plus second times second, and so on up to the last one (the $n$-th). The symbol $\sum$ ([summation](#/prerequisiti/la-sommatoria)) is just a short way of writing the same sum. The last piece says that the same number can also be obtained in another way: length of $\mathbf{a}$ times length of $\mathbf{b}$ times the cosine of the angle $\theta$ between the two vectors.

The same product can be found written in many ways, all equivalent: $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{a}^t\mathbf{b} = \langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}\rangle$, and also just $\mathbf{ab}$ if it is clear from the context.

</Callout>

What is it for? The piece with the cosine gives us a picture: the <T id="prodotto-scalare">dot product</T> measures **how much two vectors point in the same direction**. Think of two arrows starting from the same point:

- if they point the same way (they are parallel), the dot product is as large as possible;
- if they form a right angle (they are *orthogonal*, that is, perpendicular), the dot product is zero;
- if they point in opposite directions, the dot product is negative.

This idea will come back again and again. For example in linear models, where $\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x}$ measures the “alignment” between the input $\mathbf{x}$ and the weights $\mathbf{w}$.

<Deep title="Worked example: two vectors in ℝ²" kind="esempio">

Take $\mathbf{a} = (3, 1)$ and $\mathbf{b} = (1, 2)$. They are two vectors with two numbers each (we say “in $\mathbb{R}^2$”).

1. Dot product: first times first plus second times second, that is, $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = 3 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 2 = 5$.
2. Lengths: $|\mathbf{a}| = \sqrt{3^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{10} \approx 3.16$ and $|\mathbf{b}| = \sqrt{1^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt{5} \approx 2.24$.
3. Cosine of the angle: divide the dot product by the product of the lengths, $\cos\theta = 5 / \sqrt{50} \approx 0.71$. A cosine of about $0.71$ corresponds to an angle of about $45^\circ$.

The product is positive: the two vectors point “the same way”.

</Deep>

The <T id="norma">Euclidean norm</T> of a vector is simply its **length** (it is also called the *modulus*). It is computed with the Pythagorean theorem: square each number of the vector, add up the squares and take the square root.

<Formula f={F.norm2} />

It is often written more simply as $\|\mathbf{x}\|$ or $|\mathbf{x}|$. There are other ways of measuring “how big” a vector is. Two useful ones are:

- the **$L^1$ norm**, $\|\mathbf{x}\|_1 = \sum_i |x_i|$: add up the absolute values of the numbers (the absolute value $|x_i|$ is the number without the minus sign). For $(3, -4)$ it is $3 + 4 = 7$;
- the **max norm**, $\|\mathbf{x}\|_\infty = \max_i |x_i|$: take the largest of the absolute values. For $(3, -4)$ it is $4$.

For comparison, the Euclidean norm of $(3, -4)$ is $\sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$.

### Partial derivatives and gradient

Let us start from the [derivative](#/prerequisiti/la-derivata): for a function of a single variable, the derivative tells **how steep** the function is at a point, that is, by how much the result changes if we move the input by a tiny amount.

The **partial derivative** is the same idea for functions that have **several variables** as input. It works like this: choose one variable, pretend that all the others are fixed numbers (constants) and compute the ordinary derivative with respect to that variable only. For a function $f(x_1, x_2, x_3)$ with three variables there are therefore three partial derivatives, one per variable: $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}$, $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2}$, $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_3}$. The symbol $\partial$ is a rounded “d” and reminds us that it is a *partial* derivative.

<Callout type="definition" title="Gradient">

The <T id="gradiente">gradient</T> of $f$ is the vector that collects all its partial derivatives, one after the other:

<Formula f={F.gradient} />

where the $\mathbf{e}_i$ are the *unit vectors*: arrows of length $1$ that indicate the directions of the axes (one per variable), all perpendicular to each other. The symbol $\nabla$ is read “nabla”.

</Callout>

<Deep title="Worked example: computing a gradient" kind="esempio">

Let $f(x_1, x_2) = x_1^2 + 3x_1x_2$.

1. Differentiate with respect to $x_1$, treating $x_2$ as if it were a fixed number. The derivative of $x_1^2$ is $2x_1$; the derivative of $3x_1x_2$ is $3x_2$ (because $3x_2$ is just a number multiplying $x_1$). Hence $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1} = 2x_1 + 3x_2$.
2. Differentiate with respect to $x_2$, treating $x_1$ as a fixed number. The piece $x_1^2$ does not contain $x_2$, so its derivative is zero; the derivative of $3x_1x_2$ is $3x_1$. Hence $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2} = 3x_1$.
3. Put them together: $\nabla f = (2x_1 + 3x_2,\; 3x_1)$.

For example, at the point $(1, 2)$ just substitute $x_1 = 1$ and $x_2 = 2$: $\nabla f(1,2) = (2 \cdot 1 + 3 \cdot 2,\; 3 \cdot 1) = (8, 3)$.

</Deep>

The geometric meaning is fundamental for the whole course. Imagine the function as a mountain landscape, where the height at each point is the value of the function. At a point, the gradient is an arrow that **points in the direction in which the climb is steepest**, and its length (its norm) tells **how steep** it is. So:

- the gradient $\nabla f$ indicates which way the function **increases** fastest;
- its opposite, $-\nabla f$, indicates which way the function **decreases** fastest.

This is exactly the idea of <T id="discesa-del-gradiente">gradient descent</T>: to find the lowest point of a function, take many small steps downhill, each time in the direction $-\nabla f$. We will use it to train models, that is, to make as small as possible a function that measures the error.

A point where the gradient is zero (all the partial derivatives are zero, that is, the ground is flat) is called a <T id="punto-stazionario">stationary point</T>. It can be of three kinds:

- a **local minimum**: the bottom of a bowl;
- a **local maximum**: the top of a hill;
- a **saddle point**: like the center of a horse saddle, where in one direction you go up and in the other you go down.

Knowing that the gradient is zero is not enough, on its own, to tell which of the three cases we are facing.

### Probability density: the Gaussian

Many real quantities (people’s height, the errors of a measurement…) behave like this: most values lie close to a central value, and values very far from the center are rare. The mathematical model of this behavior is the **normal distribution**. It is described by two numbers:

- the **mean** $\mu$ (the Greek letter “mu”): the central value, where the peak is;
- the **variance** $\sigma^2$ (“sigma squared”): it tells how spread out the values are around the mean. The larger it is, the wider and lower the curve.

Its *probability density* is the bell-shaped curve (the “<T id="gaussiana">Gaussian</T> function”): where the curve is high, the values are more likely.

<Formula f={F.gaussian} />

**In plain words.** The formula looks complicated, but it does just one thing: it takes the distance of $x$ from the mean, $(x - \mu)$, and the larger this distance, the smaller the result becomes. The fraction in front only serves to make the total area under the curve equal to $1$ (that is, 100% probability).

With $\mu = 0$ and $\sigma^2 = 1$ one obtains the **standard normal**: the bell centered at zero and with the “reference” width.

## Summary and next steps

<Callout type="abstract" title="Summary">

ML is making a machine learn a task starting from examples. It is needed when we have a lot of data but do not know how to write the rules. The course studies it as **function approximation from examples**: the models are built one “building block” after another, looking at the principles (which stay the same even when the models change) and measuring rigorously how well they work. The key idea of the whole course is the trade-off between **model flexibility** and **complexity control**: **generalization**, that is, the ability to work well on new data, depends on it.

</Callout>

[Lessons 2–3](#/lezione/03) cover: what ML is useful for, learning as function approximation with a running example, and the pieces that make up an ML system (task, hypothesis space, inductive bias, loss function, generalization). <Exam>
<Q q="Why is ML a necessity and not a luxury? Under which conditions is it appropriate to use it?">

For two reasons. The first: there are **more and more data** to analyze (science itself becomes *data-driven*). The second: it is **hard to program by hand** an intelligent and adaptive behavior, because for many tasks we do not know how to write the rules. It is right to use it when there is **little or no prior knowledge** about the solution but it is **easy to obtain examples** with the correct result (spam, recognition of characters, faces, speech).

</Q>
<Q q="What is the gradient and why does $-\nabla f$ indicate the direction of steepest decrease?">

It is the vector of the partial derivatives, $\nabla f = (\partial f/\partial x_1, \dots, \partial f/\partial x_n)$. At every point it points in the direction of **steepest slope** (the steepest climb) and its length tells how steep it is. Why? If we take a small step $\Delta\mathbf{x}$, the function changes by about $\nabla f \cdot \Delta\mathbf{x}$ (a dot product). For a given step length, this product is as large as possible when the step has the same direction as $\nabla f$, and it is as negative as possible when the step goes the opposite way, that is, along $-\nabla f$. This is the idea of gradient descent.

</Q>
<Q q="What is a stationary point? Is it always a minimum?">

It is a point where the gradient is zero (the “ground” is flat). It is **not** always a minimum: it can be a local minimum, a local maximum or a saddle point. The mere fact that the gradient is zero is not enough to distinguish the three cases (this is clearly visible in figure 1.5, comparing “Bowl”, “Dome” and “Saddle”).

</Q>
</Exam>
