## Stopping the training

### Do not fix an arbitrary number of epochs

<Callout type="warning" title="Frequent mistake">

Avoid stopping the training of a network after a **fixed and arbitrary number of epochs**, that is, a number of passes over the data decided at random before starting (for example “I stop at 500”).

</Callout>

Why:

- if the number is small, we can stop **too early** (underfitting: the network has not learned yet). In addition we favor high learning rates, which need fewer epochs, even if they are not the best;
- if it is large, we can stop **too late** (overfitting or wasted time);
- the same value cannot be right for all the configurations and for all the iterations of a K-fold CV;
- and which number should be used in the final retraining?

**Choosing the number of epochs through model selection** is not the best practice either. It is better than a fixed number, but it is still imprecise (because of the problems with K-fold CV and because better criteria exist). The solutions that work well with a **well-regularized** system are those seen in [lesson 8](#/lezione/08):

- looking at the convergence of training, that is, stopping when the error or the gradient go below a threshold (when there is almost no improvement any more);
- or <T id="early-stopping">**early stopping**</T> (stopping when the validation error stops going down), if we can or want to use a validation set.

### Early stopping

<Callout type="warning" title="Early stopping and model selection">

The early stopping (ES) criterion is **part of model selection** (it must not be applied on the TS): deciding when to stop is a choice, like choosing a hyperparameter. In principle it requires a **VL set every time** the model is trained.

</Callout>

It is delicate for three reasons. It uses part of the data only to decide when to stop. It does not give a concrete hyperparameter value to reuse. And it can become complicated inside a CV. The problem: how do we choose the stopping point for the **final retraining**, if every fold of the CV has a different stopping point?

- We could treat the number of epochs as a hyperparameter and take the **mean** over the folds. But in the retraining the amount of data changes, and with it the best stopping point can change. For example with more data per epoch there are more updates per epoch in on-line, so fewer epochs are needed. We risk training too little or too much.
- **Much better**: take the **mean of the training error** at the best validation point and use it as a threshold in the retraining. That is: in every fold we look at what the training error was at the moment when validation was at its minimum; we take the mean of these values; in the retraining we stop when the training error reaches that value. This way we reach the same level of fitting, that is, of adaptation to the data (avoiding training too little).
- Otherwise a VL set is needed every time. For example: CV with ES active in every validation fold (together with the other hyperparameters); once the best hyperparameters have been found, we retrain on the dataset put back together, again using part of the data as VL for ES.

It is a choice: use ES or rely on **regularized models** with standard stopping criteria (e.g. no improvement on training). In any case regularization helps to avoid strong overtraining and makes us depend less on ES.

<Deep title="Why the threshold on the training error works better than the mean of the epochs" kind="intuizione">

The number of epochs does not have the same meaning with different amounts of data. In the retraining there are more data, so every epoch (in on-line) makes more updates and the same number of epochs takes the training further. The training error, instead, directly measures **how much** the model has adapted to the data. Stopping when it reaches the mean value it had at the best validation point reproduces the same level of fitting, whatever the number of epochs needed. It is like baking a cake: it is better to look at whether it is done, not at how many minutes have passed, because with a different oven the minutes change.

</Deep>

## Random initialization and model selection

Different initializations of the weights (different starting points) lead to **different models**. How do we choose the final one (a choice that must be written in the report)?

- We compute **mean and variance** of the error/accuracy over the different trials: any given case will fall in that interval, so the risk is under control. This does not hold if the variance is high, that is, if the results change a lot from one trial to another (which is a negative sign anyway, see the lesson on bias and variance).
- We can use **all** the models, with an <T id="ensemble">**ensemble**</T> (a group of models that answers together: mean of the outputs or vote).
- If we choose **one**, that is a **model selection** and a VL set is needed. The options: the **best** (minimum error on VL), the **median** (the one in the middle of the ranking: a more cautious choice), or a **random** one (in this case no VL set is needed).
- We can also discard the “unlucky” cases by looking only at the training error.

## Sequential selection of the hyperparameters

<Callout type="warning" title="Frequent mistake">

Selecting the <T id="iperparametri">hyperparameters</T> **one at a time**, in sequence, introduces a <T id="selezione-sequenziale">**bias related to the order**</T>: the result depends on the order in which they are chosen. Example: choosing the best $\eta$ with 10 units and then using it with 100 units as well. With 100 units the best $\eta$ might be another one.

</Callout>

The correct procedure:

1. initial trials to find the right **intervals** of the values (for example by looking at the stability of training) and the most **influential** hyperparameters (for example by looking at the variance of the results);
2. search on a <T id="grid-search">**grid with all the combinations**</T> of the hyperparameters (more hyperparameters and more values → larger table);
3. possible **nested** grids, from coarse to fine, on smaller and smaller intervals, but considering **all the important hyperparameters together**, to take the cross effects into account (one hyperparameter changes the effect of the other).

But: we must reason, choose the aspects that matter, and not rely only on systematic and expensive automatic approaches. The time constraints of the project should push toward simple and fast schemes, but correct and reasonable ones.

## What to do after selection and assessment?

<Callout type="question" title="Can I redesign the model based on the result on the test?">

No: it is not the correct way and it is not really advantageous (unless we have new data for a new test set). By repeating this procedure on the same data we can get any result we want, adapting the model to that dataset. The model can become perfect on it and not in future use, and **we are no longer estimating the risk**.

*We must not tune the predictor (by hand or automatically) on the basis of the test error, because we could not do so on future examples.* In competitions the <T id="blind-test-set">blind test</T> (a test whose answers are not seen) prevents exactly this. The evaluation on an internal test is still useful for **checking our own approach**.

</Callout>

The temptation to decide on the basis of the results is a “psychological” problem, external to ML methods. We must at least be aware of it, or better:

- to make a **good model** we must do a **good model selection**;
- to estimate the **real performance** we must do a **rigorous** estimate on the test.

## Cross-validation can overfit

Can an intensive use of cross-validation lead to overfitting? **Yes**, and it keeps the problems already seen. Example with <T id="feature-selection">feature selection</T> (the choice of which variables to use):

- dataset with 20–30 records (examples) and 1000 attributes (variables);
- we try 1000 linear regression models, each with a single attribute;
- the best of the 1000 looks good (on the validation set)... but it would have looked good with a **purely random** output too! Trying 1000 models, by chance one goes well.

Or (wrong use): approximating a random target we find, with a K-fold CV for the test, an error of 3% instead of the true 50%, after choosing the features with the best correlation with the targets. **Why?** The selection was made on **all** the examples, including those that later act as test (it is the [counterexample of part 1](#/lezione/09/un-controesempio-istruttivo)).

What can we do? Set the samples aside **before** selecting:

- keep an **additional test set** before any model selection and check that the best model works well on it too;
- for model selection with CV: use “**CV + test**” or <T id="double-cv">**double CV**</T> (redoing the selection for every fold);
- for assessment with CV: do the **feature selection inside every fold**, using only the training data of that fold. This way we get the correct estimate, about 50% for the random task.

## Which cross-validation?

| | Disadvantages | Advantages |
|---|---|---|
| **Test set (<T id="hold-out">hold-out</T>)** | variance: unreliable estimate of future performance (good/bad luck) | cheap |
| **<T id="leave-one-out">Leave-one-out</T>** | expensive; has some weird behavior | does not waste data |
| **10-fold** | wastes 10% of the data; 10 times more expensive than the test set | wastes only 10%; only 10 times more expensive instead of $R$ times |
| **3-fold** | wastes more data than 10-fold; more expensive than the test set | slightly better than the test set |
| **R-fold** ($R = l$) | identical to leave-one-out | |

How to read it: “wasting” data means not using them for training, because in that round they are needed for evaluation. In 10-fold every model is trained on 90% of the data, so 10% is “wasted”. $R$ is the number of examples: leave-one-out requires one training run for every example.

### Example 3: K-NN

Why **leave-one-out** for K-NN and 10-fold for networks? For **computational** reasons, that is, of cost. For K-NN (and the other nonparametric methods) LOOCV (leave-one-out CV) costs as much as a normal prediction: there is no model to train, it is enough to remove one point and look for its neighbors among the others.

And why stop at $K = 6$? Only because things seemed to get worse as $K$ grew. And there is no guarantee that a local optimum of $K$ with respect to LOOCV (a value better than its neighbors) is the global one (the best of all): the relation can be very irregular. To explore many values we can use exponentially growing values ($K = 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, \dots$) or a *hill-climbing* search (we start from an initial value and move toward the nearby values as long as we improve).

## Practical conclusions

- **Simple hold-out** is widely used: sometimes the test set is already given separately or “taken” from earlier applications. Be careful when the data are few, and check the error rates on TR and VL.
- **5- or 10-fold CV** is recommended as a good compromise (between bias and variance of the estimate) compared with leave-one-out (Hastie et al.).

In the [next lesson](#/lezione/12) we go back to the **analytical** estimate of the expected error (with a formula instead of trials on the data), with Structural Risk Minimization and the VC-dimension. It offers interesting insights for the selection and the comparison of models (where what matters is the **relative size** of the errors, that is, which model is less wrong than the other, more than the exact value), as an alternative to purely empirical evaluation.

<Exam>
<Q q="Why should we not stop training after a fixed number of epochs?">

A small number stops too early (underfitting, and it favors high learning rates that converge in fewer epochs); a large one stops too late (overfitting or wasted time). No value is right for all the configurations and for all the iterations of a K-fold CV, and we do not know which one to use in the final retraining. Choosing the epochs with model selection is imprecise too: with a well-regularized model a convergence criterion (threshold on the decrease of the error or on the gradient) or early stopping with a validation set is better.

</Q>
<Q q="How is early stopping handled with cross-validation and the final retraining?">

Early stopping is part of model selection (never on the TS) and requires a VL at every training run. In CV every fold has a different stopping point: the mean of the epochs is risky, because with more data the best point changes. It is much better to use as the threshold of the retraining the mean of the training error at the best validation point, so as to get the same level of fitting. Alternatively a VL is kept in the final retraining too. Or we give up ES and use regularized models with standard stopping criteria.

</Q>
<Q q="How do we choose the final model with several random initializations?">

We report mean and variance of the results over the trials (any given case falls in the interval; a high variance is a negative sign). All the models can be used in an ensemble (mean of the outputs or vote). Choosing one is a model selection that requires a VL: the best on VL, the median (more cautious) or a random one (with no VL). The unlucky cases can be discarded by looking only at the training error.

</Q>
<Q q="What is the mistake of sequential hyperparameter selection?">

Choosing the hyperparameters one at a time, keeping the previous ones fixed, introduces a bias related to the order and ignores the cross effects: for example the best $\eta$ found with 10 units may not be the best with 100. The correct procedure: initial trials to find intervals and influential hyperparameters, then a grid with all the combinations, possibly nested from coarse to fine but always on all the important hyperparameters together.

</Q>
<Q q="Can cross-validation overfit? How can it be avoided?">

Yes: trying a great many models (e.g. 1000 regressions with a single attribute on 20–30 records) the best one looks good in validation even with a random output; and if the features are chosen on all the examples, a K-fold for the test gives 3% instead of the true 50%. It is avoided by setting the data aside before selecting: an additional test set before any model selection; for selection with CV the CV + test scheme or double CV (selection redone for every fold); for assessment with CV feature selection inside every fold.

</Q>
<Q q="Compare hold-out, K-fold and leave-one-out.">

Hold-out is cheap but the estimate has high variance (it depends on the luck of the split). Leave-one-out does not waste data but it is expensive and can behave strangely; for K-NN it costs only one prediction per example and is therefore convenient. 10-fold wastes 10% of the data and costs 10 times hold-out instead of $l$ times; 3-fold wastes more and is only slightly better than hold-out. 5- or 10-fold is the recommended compromise between bias and variance of the estimate.

</Q>
</Exam>
