## Neural networks as an ML tool

Neural networks are studied with two different aims:

- **modeling biological systems** and learning processes, that is, understanding how the brain really works (philosophy, psychology, neurobiology, cognitive science, computational neuroscience): here **biological realism** (faithfulness to biology) is essential;
- **building effective ML systems and algorithms** (statistics, AI, physics, mathematics, engineering), often giving up strict faithfulness to biology: here what matters are the **computational and algorithmic** properties, that is, what can be computed and how.

In the course we care about the second aim: **ANNs** (Artificial Neural Networks) as a **flexible ML tool**, in the sense of function approximation. A network implements a mathematical [function](#/prerequisiti/funzioni) $h(\mathbf{x})$ with special properties: it takes an input $\mathbf{x}$ and returns an answer.

Neural networks are a “historical machine”, but they remain a powerful approach:

- they learn from examples;
- they are **universal approximators** (Cybenko’s theorem): they are flexible enough to imitate any function, even nonlinear ones (that is, ones that are not straight lines);
- they handle noise and incomplete data, and when conditions get worse their performance **degrades gracefully**: it drops a little at a time, it does not collapse all at once;
- they deal with real data (numbers with decimals) and discrete data (separate values, such as categories), both for regression and for classification;
- they are not a single model but a **paradigm**, that is, a whole family of models (in the class of sub-symbolic approaches, those that work with numbers and not with written rules).

The underlying “philosophy” is <T id="connessionismo">**connectionism**</T>: complex behaviors (mental, behavioral) that **emerge**, that is, arise by themselves, from the interaction of many simple computing units connected to each other. No single unit is intelligent; the whole is.

## The biological inspiration

The human brain has more than $10^{10}$ neurons (ten billion), each with $10^4$–$10^5$ connections (from ten thousand to a hundred thousand). A neuron responds in about one millisecond. Yet we recognize an image in about 0.1 seconds, that is, 100 milliseconds. In that time there is room for at most about a hundred **serial** computation steps (one after the other). A hundred steps are very few for such a hard task. It follows that the brain must perform a **massively parallel** computation: a great many neurons work at the same moment. (Note that face recognition, so easy for us, is complex for conventional computers.)

How it works, in short:

- the neuron goes through cycles of charge and discharge (thanks to sodium and potassium ions, particles with an electric charge);
- the inputs arrive from the dendrites (the input “branches”) and change the potential of the cell, that is, its electric charge;
- when the potential exceeds a **threshold** (a limit value), the neuron generates a **spike**: a voltage pulse;
- information is also encoded in the time intervals between one spike and the next.

The signal travels along the axon (the output “cable”) to the **synapses**, the points of contact with the next neuron. There it passes in chemical form, through substances called **neurotransmitters**. Synapses can be **excitatory** (they push the next neuron to fire) or **inhibitory** (they hold it back). Above all, they change with learning: their strength (the “weight”) is reinforced in response to stimuli. This is the **plasticity** of the nervous system, that is, its ability to modify itself.

<Callout type="definition" title="Hebbian learning (Hebb, 1949)">

A synapse gets stronger when the inputs of the neuron (and the related outputs) are repeated. The incoming stimulus reinforces the synapse, so **the weights move closer to the inputs** (<T id="hebb">Hebbian learning</T>). In short: a connection that is used often becomes stronger.

</Callout>

## The artificial neuron

<Figure n="6.2" title="The processing unit" size="wide">

  <Caption>The processing unit (artificial neuron) $i$: the inputs $x_1, x_2, x_3$ and the constant input $x_0 = 1$ enter with the weights $w_{i1}, w_{i2}, w_{i3}$ and the bias $w_{i0}$; the node computes the weighted sum $\Sigma$ and applies the function $f$ to it. The thickness of each connection is proportional to the weight (blue positive, orange negative). Move inputs and weights and choose $f$.</Caption>

A <T id="unita">**unit**</T> (also called node or neuron) receives inputs from external sources or from other units. Every connection has a **weight** $w$: a number that says how much that input counts. The weight is a free parameter, that is, a number that learning can modify (it is the analog of the strength of the synapse). Unit $i$ computes:

<Formula f={F.unit} />

**In plain words.** The unit takes two steps. First: it multiplies every input $x_j$ by its weight $w_{ij}$ and adds everything up (the symbol $\sum$ is the [summation](#/prerequisiti/la-sommatoria)); the result is called $net_i$. Second: it passes this number through a function $f$ and gets the output $o_i$. Example: with inputs $x_1 = 2$, $x_2 = 1$, weights $w_{i1} = 0.5$, $w_{i2} = -1$ and bias $w_{i0} = 0.3$, we get $net_i = 0.3 \cdot 1 + 0.5 \cdot 2 - 1 \cdot 1 = 0.3$.

- The weighted sum $net_i$ is the **net input** of unit $i$. It also contains the bias $w_{i0}$, thanks to the trick of the constant input $x_0 = 1$ already seen with linear models.
- $f$ is the <T id="funzione-di-attivazione">**activation function**</T>: the rule that turns the net input into the output (it can be linear, threshold, sigmoidal…).

<Callout type="warning" title="Notation for the weights">

$w_{ij}$ is the weight **of unit $i$**, that is, of the connection **from input/unit $j$ to unit $i$** (not the other way round). The first index is the one that receives, the second is the one that sends. Some sources (for example Wikipedia in the section on backpropagation, and some libraries) use the opposite convention; in the course we always use the traditional one: the first index is the receiving unit.

</Callout>

Three classical activation functions:

<Figure n="6.3" title="Three activation functions">
  <Activations />
  <Caption>Linear (identity), threshold (Perceptron) and logistic (sigmoid) activation functions, applied to the same net input. Drag net along the axis of one of the three plots.</Caption>

1. **linear** (identity): the output is equal to the net input, unchanged, $h(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_i w_i x_i$;
2. **threshold** (step): the output jumps from one value to the other when the net input exceeds the threshold. It is the **Perceptron**, that is, the LTU already seen;
3. **logistic** (sigmoid): a smooth S-shaped curve; see below.

## The Perceptron

The <T id="perceptron">**Perceptron**</T> was proposed by Frank Rosenblatt (1957–1960) to classify patterns (that is, to recognize which group an input belongs to), imitating human perception. The idea: an “eye” made of photocells (light sensors) connected to units that compute a weighted sum and compare it with a threshold.

<Figure n="6.4" title="Rosenblatt’s Perceptron" size="wide">
  <Rosenblatt />
  <Caption>The original Perceptron, used to recognize letters: a letter projected onto a grid of photocells, association units that look at parts of the image and a threshold response unit. Here the association units are 2 × 2 blocks and the response unit distinguishes X from O with weights learned by the Perceptron algorithm (seen below).</Caption>
</Figure>

The single neuron is a very simple computing unit. Minsky: *“for the kind of recognition it can do, it is such a simple machine that it would be astonishing if nature did not make use of it somewhere”*. The Perceptron has historical importance and is **paradigmatic** (that is, it is the reference model) for the step from linear models to nonlinear ones: by putting together and connecting many perceptrons we get **MLP** (Multi-Layer Perceptron) networks.

### McCulloch and Pitts networks (1943)

The first model, *“A logical calculus of the ideas immanent in nervous activity”* (1943), asked two questions: what does a given network compute? And can a network compute a given logical formula?

- Neurons have two states: active (1) and not active (0).
- All synapses are equivalent and are described by a real number (the strength $w$): positive for **excitatory** connections, negative for **inhibitory** ones.
- A neuron $i$ becomes active when the sum of the weights $w_{ij}$ coming from the active neurons $j$, plus a bias, exceeds zero.

Inputs and outputs are binary (only 0 or 1): in essence it is the LTU, that is, the Perceptron.

#### Representing Boolean functions

A Boolean function takes inputs that are 0 (false) or 1 (true) and answers 0 or 1. We use $net = \mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x} = \sum_{i=0}^n w_i x_i$ and the rule: output $1$ if $net > 0$, $0$ (or $-1$) otherwise. Then:

- **AND** (true only if both inputs are true): $w_1 = w_2 = 1$, $w_0 = -1.5$ (both inputs must be 1 to exceed $1.5$). Check: with $x_1 = x_2 = 1$ the net is $1 + 1 - 1.5 = 0.5 > 0$; with only one input at 1 it is $1 - 1.5 = -0.5$, which is not positive;
- **OR** (true if at least one input is true): $w_1 = w_2 = 1$, $w_0 = -0.5$ (one input at 1 is enough). Check: with only one input at 1 the net is $1 - 0.5 = 0.5 > 0$; with both at 0 it is $-0.5$.

#### XOR and two-layer networks

**XOR** (“exclusive or”) is true when only one of the two inputs is true. It is not linearly separable: there is no straight line that divides the true cases from the false ones. So no single perceptron can represent it (Minsky and Papert, 1969). It can, however, be broken down into simpler pieces:

$$
x_1 \oplus x_2 = x_1\bar{x}_2 + \bar{x}_1 x_2 = \bar{h}_1 \cdot h_2, \qquad \text{with } h_1 = x_1 \cdot x_2 \text{ (AND)}, \quad h_2 = x_1 + x_2 \text{ (OR)}.
$$

How to read it: $\oplus$ is XOR; the bar over a letter means NOT (“the opposite of”); the dot $\cdot$ is AND and $+$ is OR. In words: XOR is true when OR is true ($h_2$: at least one) but AND is false ($\bar h_1$: not both).

<Callout type="theorem" title="XOR as a composition of AND and OR">

Here $\wedge$ means AND and $\vee$ means OR (see [logic](#/prerequisiti/logica-e-conteggi)). De Morgan’s law says that “not (a and b)” is the same thing as “(not a) or (not b)”: $\overline{h_1} = \overline{a \wedge b} = \bar a \vee \bar b$. Hence

$$
\bar h_1 \wedge h_2 = (\bar a \vee \bar b) \wedge (a \vee b) = (\bar a \wedge a) \vee (\bar a \wedge b) \vee (a \wedge \bar b) \vee (b \wedge \bar b) = (\bar a \wedge b) \vee (a \wedge \bar b) = a \oplus b.
$$

In the second step the two brackets are multiplied out, piece by piece. The terms $\bar a \wedge a$ and $b \wedge \bar b$ are always false (a thing cannot be true and false together), so they disappear. What remains is “b but not a” or “a but not b”: that is exactly XOR.

</Callout>

In this way we build a **two-layer** network. Two hidden units compute $h_1$ (AND) and $h_2$ (OR). An output unit computes $\bar h_1 \wedge h_2$ (weights $-1$ from $h_1$ and $+1$ from $h_2$, bias $-0.5$).

### The hidden layer as a representation

The figure shows the key concept. The <T id="strato-nascosto">hidden layer</T> (the units in the middle, between input and output) produces an **internal re-representation** of the inputs: it rewrites the data in a new form. Take the point $(1,0)$, which in the original space sits in the bottom right corner. For it AND is false and OR is true, so it becomes $h_1 = 0$, $h_2 = 1$. The same happens to $(0,1)$. So the two positive points $(0,1)$ and $(1,0)$ are mapped **to the same point** $(0,1)$ of the hidden space, and the problem becomes linearly separable for the output unit: now one line is enough.

<Callout type="tip" title="The most important concept of neural networks">

Developing **high-level features** (more elaborate properties of the data) in the hidden layers is the key factor of neural networks: the representation in the hidden layer makes the task of the output layer easier. This composition of intermediate operations can be extended over **many layers of abstraction**, one on top of the other. And there is an important difference from what we saw here, where the weights were chosen by hand: in neural networks these representations **are learned** from the data. This is the idea behind *representation learning* and **deep learning**.

</Callout>

<Callout type="abstract" title="Properties of perceptron networks">

- Perceptrons represent AND, OR, NOT (and therefore NAND, NOR) → **every Boolean function** can be represented by a network of perceptrons.
- **Two levels** are enough (more levels can be more efficient, that is, use fewer units).
- A single layer is not enough (Minsky and Papert, 1969): the limits come from linear separability.
- So far, however, **we have no learning algorithm**, not even for a single unit: we set the weights by hand.

</Callout>

## Learning for a single unit

Historically there are two families of methods:

1. <T id="adaline">**Adaline**</T> (Adaptive Linear Neuron, by Widrow and Hoff): during training the unit is **linear** (with no threshold). We use the direct LMS solution or gradient descent, exactly as in the [lecture on linear models](#/lezione/05/il-problema-di-apprendimento). It is used for regression and, by adding the threshold at the end, for classification. It is the approach we will generalize to MLPs.
2. **Perceptron** (Rosenblatt): during training the unit is **nonlinear** (it has the threshold function). It is used only for classification; its capabilities and its convergence are studied.

So to build a linear classifier we have three algorithms: direct LMS, gradient-based LMS and now the Perceptron algorithm (and others will come, such as SVMs).

### The Perceptron learning algorithm

The goal is to **minimize the number of misclassified patterns**: to find weights $\mathbf{w}$ for which $\operatorname{sign}(\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x}) = d$, that is, for which the sign of the weighted sum coincides with the right answer $d$. It is an **on-line** algorithm: it takes one step for every pattern, one at a time.

<Callout type="abstract" title="Perceptron Learning Algorithm">

1. Initialize the weights (to zero or to small random values).
2. Choose a learning rate $\eta \in (0, 1)$, that is, a number between 0 and 1 that decides how large each correction is.
3. Until the stopping condition is met (e.g. the weights no longer change), for every pattern $(\mathbf{x}, d)$ with $d \in \{+1, -1\}$:
   - compute $out = \operatorname{sign}(\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x})$, the current answer;
   - if $out = d$ (right answer), **do not change the weights**;
   - if $out \ne d$ (wrong answer), update: $\mathbf{w}_{new} = \mathbf{w} + \eta\, d\, \mathbf{x}$.

</Callout>

<Formula f={F.perceptronRule} />

**In plain words.** We correct only when we make a mistake. The correction consists in adding to the weights a small piece of the misclassified pattern: $\eta$ decides how large the piece is, $d$ decides the direction (we add if the right answer was $+1$, we subtract if it was $-1$).

In practice we add $\eta\mathbf{x}$ if $\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x} \le 0$ and $d = +1$ (false negative: the model said “no” but it was “yes”), we subtract $\eta\mathbf{x}$ if $\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x} > 0$ and $d = -1$ (false positive: it said “yes” but it was “no”). The same rule can be written in another form:

$$
\mathbf{w}_{new} = \mathbf{w} + \tfrac{1}{2}\eta\,(d - out)\,\mathbf{x},
$$

because $d - out$ is $0$ if the classification is correct and $\pm 2$ if it is wrong. Indeed, if $d = +1$ and $out = -1$ the difference is $2$; if $d = -1$ and $out = +1$ it is $-2$. The factor $\tfrac12$ serves to bring that 2 back to 1.

<Callout type="warning" title="Difference from LMS">

The formula looks like the LMS one, but here $out$ contains the **sign**: $out = \operatorname{sign}(\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x})$, that is, it is only $+1$ or $-1$. In LMS, instead, the error was computed on $\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x}$ (with no threshold), which is any number.

</Callout>

### Delta rule: two readings

- The form $\mathbf{w}_{new} = \mathbf{w} + \eta\, d\,\mathbf{x}$ can be read as **Hebbian learning**: $\mathbf{w}$ moves toward $\mathbf{x}$ (the weights move closer to the inputs).
- The form $\mathbf{w}_{new} = \mathbf{w} + \eta\,(d - out)\,\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{w} + \eta\,\delta\,\mathbf{x}$ is an **error-correction** rule (<T id="delta-rule">delta rule</T>, Widrow-Hoff): the weight changes in proportion to the error $\delta$. Large error, large correction; zero error, no correction.

In terms of neurons: *the change of a synaptic weight is proportional to the product of the error signal and the input signal that excites the synapse*. The computation is easy when the error signal $\delta$ is **directly measurable**, that is, when we know the desired answer for the unit. (This will be the problem in networks with hidden layers: for the units in the middle we do not know the desired answer.)

### Representing and learning

These are two different things. **Representing** means: there exist weights that give the right answer. **Learning** means: we have a procedure that finds those weights. The Perceptron can **represent** linear decision boundaries (straight lines), so it solves linearly separable problems. But does it always manage to **learn** the solution? Yes: the Perceptron with its algorithm is always able to learn what it can represent.

<Callout type="note" title="A historic result">

The convergence theorem is a milestone: a model **inspired by biology**, with well-defined computing capabilities that are **proved mathematically**.

</Callout>

### The Perceptron convergence theorem

<Callout type="theorem" title="Perceptron convergence theorem">

If the problem is **linearly separable**, the Perceptron algorithm converges (that is, it comes to classify all the patterns correctly and stops) in a **finite number of steps**, whatever the starting point. The final solution, however, is not unique and depends on the starting point (<T id="convergenza-perceptron">convergence theorem</T>).

If the problem is not separable, the algorithm can be unstable: it develops **cycles** (it keeps repeating the same corrections without stopping), with weights that are not necessarily optimal.

</Callout>

In the proof we do not write the $T$ in the [dot products](#/prerequisiti/prodotto-scalare) between vectors: $\mathbf{w}\mathbf{x}$ means $\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x}$.

#### Preliminaries 1: reducing to all-positive patterns

Let $(\mathbf{x}_i, d_i)$ be the training examples, with $d_i \in \{+1, -1\}$ and $i = 1,\dots,l$. If the problem is linearly separable there exists a solution $\mathbf{w}^*$ such that

$$
d_i(\mathbf{w}^*\mathbf{x}_i) \ge \alpha, \qquad \text{with } \alpha = \min_i d_i(\mathbf{w}^*\mathbf{x}_i) > 0.
$$

How to read it: for a correctly classified pattern, $\mathbf{w}^*\mathbf{x}_i$ has the same sign as $d_i$, so the product $d_i(\mathbf{w}^*\mathbf{x}_i)$ is positive. The larger it is, the farther the point is from the boundary. $\alpha$ (the Greek letter “alpha”) is the smallest of these values: the margin of the point closest to the boundary.

Hence $\mathbf{w}^*(d_i\mathbf{x}_i) \ge \alpha$ (we only moved $d_i$ inside the bracket). Define $\mathbf{x}'_i = d_i\mathbf{x}_i$: it is the pattern itself if $d_i = +1$, the “flipped” pattern (with all signs changed) if $d_i = -1$. Then **$\mathbf{w}^*$ is a solution of the original problem if and only if it is a solution of the problem $(\mathbf{x}'_i, +1)$**, in which all the targets are positive:

- (if) $\mathbf{w}^*$ solves the original problem $\Rightarrow d_i(\mathbf{w}^*\mathbf{x}_i) \ge \alpha \Rightarrow \mathbf{w}^*\mathbf{x}'_i \ge \alpha \Rightarrow \mathbf{w}^*$ solves $(\mathbf{x}'_i, +1)$;
- (only if) the reverse reasoning.

So it is enough to prove the theorem for a problem with **positive patterns only**: “flipping” the negative patterns changes nothing.

#### Preliminaries 2: form of the weights after $q$ errors

Assume $\mathbf{w}(0) = \mathbf{0}$ (we start from weights that are all zero), $\eta = 1$ and define $\beta = \max_i \|\mathbf{x}_i\|^2$: it is the squared [length](#/prerequisiti/lunghezza-e-distanza) of the longest pattern ($\beta$ is the Greek letter “beta”). With positive patterns only, the rule becomes $\mathbf{w}(j) = \mathbf{w}(j-1) + \mathbf{x}_{i_j}$ at every error: we add the misclassified pattern. After $q$ errors (all false negatives):

$$
\mathbf{w}(q) = \sum_{j=1}^{q} \mathbf{x}_{i_j},
$$

that is, the weights are simply the sum of all the patterns on which a mistake was made. Here $i_j$ denotes the misclassified patterns (for example the indices 2, 8, 9, 14 of the training set; the same pattern can appear more than once).

#### Idea of the proof

We look at the squared length of the weights, $\|\mathbf{w}(q)\|^2$, after $q$ errors. Two things are found:

- a **lower bound** (a value it cannot go below) that grows like $q^2$;
- an **upper bound** (a value it cannot go above) that grows like $q$.

A quadratic function ($q^2$) sooner or later overtakes a linear one ($q$). But the lower bound cannot lie above the upper one. So the two bounds agree only as long as $q$ does not exceed a certain maximum value: the number of errors (and therefore of updates) is finite.

#### Lower bound

We take the dot product between the solution $\mathbf{w}^*$ and the weights after $q$ errors:

$$
\mathbf{w}^{*T}\mathbf{w}(q) = \mathbf{w}^{*T}\sum_{j=1}^{q}\mathbf{x}_{i_j} \ge q\alpha,
$$

because every term $\mathbf{w}^{*T}\mathbf{x}_{i_j}$ is at least $\alpha$, and there are $q$ terms. Now we need the **Cauchy-Schwarz inequality**: $\|\mathbf{a}\|^2\|\mathbf{b}\|^2 \ge (\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{b})^2$. We use it with $\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{w}^*$ and $\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{w}(q)$:

$$
\|\mathbf{w}^*\|^2\,\|\mathbf{w}(q)\|^2 \ge \big(\mathbf{w}^{*T}\mathbf{w}(q)\big)^2 \ge (q\alpha)^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \|\mathbf{w}(q)\|^2 \ge \frac{(q\alpha)^2}{\|\mathbf{w}^*\|^2}.
$$

In the last step we divided by $\|\mathbf{w}^*\|^2$. The result contains $q^2$: the lower bound grows quadratically.

<Deep title="How to read the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality" kind="come-si-legge">

Since $\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{b} = \|\mathbf{a}\|\,\|\mathbf{b}\|\cos\theta$ (the [dot product](#/lezione/01/prodotto-scalare-e-norme), where $\theta$ is the angle between the two vectors), the inequality only says that $\cos^2\theta \le 1$: the dot product cannot exceed the product of the lengths. In the proof it is used “the other way round”: if the dot product $\mathbf{w}^{*T}\mathbf{w}(q)$ grows at least like $q\alpha$, then the length of $\mathbf{w}(q)$ must also grow at least proportionally.

</Deep>

#### Upper bound

We use the formula for the square of a sum, which also holds for vectors: $\|\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{a}\|^2 + 2\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b} + \|\mathbf{b}\|^2$. The weights after the $q$-th error are the previous ones plus the misclassified pattern, so:

$$
\|\mathbf{w}(q)\|^2 = \|\mathbf{w}(q-1) + \mathbf{x}_{i_q}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{w}(q-1)\|^2 + 2\,\mathbf{w}(q-1)\,\mathbf{x}_{i_q} + \|\mathbf{x}_{i_q}\|^2.
$$

The pattern $\mathbf{x}_{i_q}$ was misclassified (it is the $q$-th error). Since its target is positive, “misclassified” means $\mathbf{w}(q-1)\,\mathbf{x}_{i_q} \le 0$. The middle term is therefore zero or negative: if we remove it, the result can only grow. We get

$$
\|\mathbf{w}(q)\|^2 \le \|\mathbf{w}(q-1)\|^2 + \|\mathbf{x}_{i_q}\|^2,
$$

that is, at every error the squared length grows at most by the squared length of one pattern. Repeating the same reasoning for all the $q$ errors (and starting from $\mathbf{w}(0) = \mathbf{0}$):

$$
\|\mathbf{w}(q)\|^2 \le \sum_{j=1}^{q}\|\mathbf{x}_{i_j}\|^2 \le q\beta.
$$

The last step holds because every pattern has squared length at most $\beta$. The result contains $q$: the upper bound grows linearly.

#### Conclusion

Putting the two bounds together:

<Formula f={F.convergence} />

**In plain words.** $\|\mathbf{w}(q)\|^2$ is squeezed between the two bounds: from below $q^2\alpha^2/\|\mathbf{w}^*\|^2$, from above $q\beta$. For them to be compatible we need “lower bound $\le$ upper bound”. Dividing both sides by $q$ and isolating $q$ we find that $q$ cannot exceed $\beta\|\mathbf{w}^*\|^2/\alpha^2$, which is a fixed number.

The number of errors (and therefore of updates) is bounded by a constant: the algorithm converges in a **finite number of steps**. $\blacksquare$ (The small black square marks the end of the proof.)

<Callout type="tip" title="Interpreting the bound">

The number of steps grows with $\beta$ (how “large” the patterns are) and decreases with $\alpha^2$: $\alpha$ measures how “well separated” the problem is (the minimum margin of the solution $\mathbf{w}^*$, that is, the distance of the point closest to the boundary). If the classes lie close to the separating hyperplane, $\alpha$ is small and more steps are needed. The idea of **margin** will become central again in SVMs.

</Callout>

## Perceptron versus LMS

The two rules look similar ($\mathbf{w}_{new} = \mathbf{w} + \eta\,\delta\,\mathbf{x}$), but the error $\delta$ is computed in a different way:

- LMS is derived (through the gradient) **without** a threshold function, minimizing the error of the linear unit: $\delta = d - \mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x}$;
- the Perceptron uses the thresholded output: $\delta = d - \operatorname{sign}(\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x})$.

Of course the model trained with LMS can be used to classify by applying the threshold at the end ($h(\mathbf{x}) = \operatorname{sign}(\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x})$, LTU).

An important consequence: **LMS can misclassify even linearly separable problems**. The reason: LMS does not minimize the number of errors of the LTU, but the squared error, and it modifies the weights **even for patterns that are already classified correctly**. Example: a pattern with target $+1$ and $\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x} = 5$ is on the right side, but for LMS the error is $1 - 5 = -4$, which squared makes 16. A pattern that is correct but “far” from the hyperplane (with $\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x}$ much greater than 1) therefore produces a large squared error that “pulls” the hyperplane toward itself, and so the hyperplane can get other points wrong.

<Figure n="6.9" title="LMS and Perceptron on a separable problem" size="wide">
  <LmsVsPerceptron />
  <Caption>A separable problem: the LMS solution (violet) misclassifies one training point, while the Perceptron (red, two different initializations: solid and dashed line) separates everything (as in Hastie et al., Fig. 4.14). Drag the far pattern of class +1.</Caption>
</Figure>

| Perceptron Learning Algorithm | LMS |
|---|---|
| Minimizes the misclassifications ($out = \operatorname{sign}(\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x})$) | Minimizes $E(\mathbf{w})$ with $out = \mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x}$ |
| Always converges for separable problems, in a finite number of steps, to a perfect classifier | **Asymptotic** convergence (it gets closer and closer with no last step), even for non-separable problems |
| Does not converge if the problem is not separable | Not always zero classification errors, even on separable problems |
| Hard to extend to networks of units | **Extensible to networks** with the gradient-based approach |

The last row is decisive: to build neural networks we need an approach based on the **gradient**, and the [gradient](#/prerequisiti/derivate-parziali-e-gradiente) requires differentiable functions, that is, functions whose [derivative](#/prerequisiti/la-derivata) can be computed.

## Sigmoidal activation functions

Besides the linear and the threshold ones, we use **nonlinear “squashing”** functions: they take any number and “squash” it into a bounded interval. The best known is the <T id="sigmoide">**logistic sigmoid**</T>, which gives continuous values in the interval $[0,1]$:

<Formula f={F.logistic} />

**In plain words.** $e$ is the number of the [exponential](#/prerequisiti/esponenziale-e-logaritmo) (about 2.718). If $x$ is very large, $e^{-ax}$ is almost zero and the function is almost $1$. If $x$ is very negative, $e^{-ax}$ is huge and the function is almost $0$. For $x = 0$ we have $e^0 = 1$, so the function is $1/2$. In between, the curve rises gently: it is an S.

It is a **smooth, differentiable version of the threshold function**: it does the same job as the step, but without the abrupt jump. The parameter $a$ is the **slope**: it says how steep the rise is.

<Figure n="6.10" title="Logistic sigmoid and hyperbolic tangent" size="wide">
  <Sigmoids />
  <Caption>Logistic sigmoid (left) for $a = 0.5$ (green), $1$ (red), $2$ (blue) and hyperbolic tangent (right, in red with $a = 1$). The dashed violet curve uses the slope chosen with the slider; the gray line is the tangent at zero (the semi-linear region).</Caption>
</Figure>

The symmetric version is the <T id="tanh">**hyperbolic tangent**</T> ($\tanh$), with values in $[-1, +1]$ instead of $[0,1]$:

$$
f_{symm}(x) = 2f_\sigma(x) - 1 = \tanh(ax/2).
$$

That is: we take the logistic, double it (from $[0,1]$ to $[0,2]$) and subtract 1 (from $[0,2]$ to $[-1,1]$).

Close to zero the sigmoid is almost a straight line (**semi-linearity**). As $a$ varies:

- for $a \to 0$ (slope tending to zero) the function tends to a **linear** function (very flat);
- for $a \to \infty$ (huge slope) it tends to the **step** function (LTU).

For classification, with the logistic an output $\ge 0.5$ corresponds to the positive class and $< 0.5$ to the zero/negative class (for tanh the threshold is 0). The threshold can be moved (studying the effect on false positives/negatives or with a ROC curve). We can also define a **rejection zone** around the threshold: if the output falls there, the model does not answer, to avoid fragile decisions (taken by a hair).

### Other activation functions

- **Radial basis functions** (RBF): $f(x) = e^{-ax^2}$ applied to $\|\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{x}_{input}\|$, the distance between weights and input. It is a Gaussian (a “bell”) centered on the weights: the output is high when the input is close to the weights → **RBF networks**.
- **Softmax**: for the case with multiple outputs (see later lectures).
- **Stochastic neurons** (that is, random): output $+1$ with probability $P(net)$, $-1$ otherwise → **Boltzmann machines** and models from statistical mechanics.
- **Piecewise linear** approximations of tanh (made of pieces of straight lines), for efficient computation (even with binary shifts).
- <T id="relu">**ReLU**</T> (Rectified Linear Unit): $f(x) = \max(0, x)$, that is, zero for negative inputs and the input itself for positive ones. It has become the default choice for deep models; it will be discussed in deep learning.
- **Softplus**, its smooth approximation: $f(x) = \ln(1 + e^x)$ ($\ln$ is the natural [logarithm](#/prerequisiti/esponenziale-e-logaritmo)).

Many activation functions have similar performance in practice.

### Derivatives

The [derivative](#/prerequisiti/la-derivata) says how much the output changes if the input changes a little. It is needed for gradient descent.

- The derivative of the identity is 1.
- The derivative of the step function **is not defined** (at zero, where the jump is) and is zero elsewhere (where the function is flat): this is exactly why it is not used with LMS. A derivative that is always zero does not say in which direction to move.
- For the sigmoids (with $a = 1$):

$$
\frac{df_\sigma(x)}{dx} = f_\sigma(x)\big(1 - f_\sigma(x)\big), \qquad \frac{d\tanh(x)}{dx} = 1 - \tanh^2(x).
$$

(With a generic $a$: $\frac{df_\sigma}{dx} = a\,f_\sigma(x)(1 - f_\sigma(x))$.)

Example: at $x = 0$ the logistic is $0.5$, so the derivative is $0.5 \cdot (1 - 0.5) = 0.25$. This is its highest value.

<Callout type="note" title="Deriving the derivative of the logistic">

$f_\sigma(x) = (1 + e^{-x})^{-1}$, so $f'_\sigma(x) = \frac{e^{-x}}{(1 + e^{-x})^2} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}} \cdot \frac{e^{-x}}{1 + e^{-x}} = f_\sigma(x)\big(1 - f_\sigma(x)\big)$, because $\frac{e^{-x}}{1 + e^{-x}} = 1 - \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}$. The steps: first we differentiate with the [chain rule](#/prerequisiti/la-regola-della-catena), then we split the fraction into two factors; the first is the logistic itself, the second is “one minus the logistic”. This is handy: the derivative is computed from the value of the function itself, with no other calculation.

</Callout>

### LMS with a sigmoidal unit

The logistic is a smooth, differentiable threshold. So we can derive an LMS algorithm by computing the gradient of the squared error, as for linear units. Only the output changes: we go from $o(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{w}$ to $o(\mathbf{x}) = f_\sigma(\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{w})$ (the weighted sum goes through the sigmoid), and we minimize

$$
E(\mathbf{w}) = \sum_{p=1}^{l}\big(d_p - f_\sigma(\mathbf{x}_p^T\mathbf{w})\big)^2.
$$

The gradient is obtained with the **chain rule**, $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial g}\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}$: when a quantity depends on another through an intermediate step $g$, we multiply the derivatives of the two steps. Here there are two intermediate steps: first $g = net$ and then $g = out$. Indeed the weight changes the net, the net changes the output, the output changes the error. For a pattern $p$ with $net_p = \mathbf{x}_p^T\mathbf{w}$:

$$
\frac{\partial E_p}{\partial w_j} = \frac{\partial E_p}{\partial o_p}\cdot\frac{\partial o_p}{\partial net_p}\cdot\frac{\partial net_p}{\partial w_j} = -2(d_p - o_p)\cdot f'_\sigma(net_p)\cdot x_{p,j}.
$$

The three pieces, one at a time:

1. how much the error changes if the output changes: $-2(d_p - o_p)$ (derivative of a square);
2. how much the output changes if the net changes: $f'_\sigma(net_p)$ (the derivative of the sigmoid);
3. how much the net changes if the weight $w_j$ changes: $x_{p,j}$ (the input attached to that weight).

Summing over all the patterns:

$$
\frac{\partial E(\mathbf{w})}{\partial w_j} = -2\sum_{p=1}^{l}\big(d_p - o(\mathbf{x}_p)\big)\, f'_\sigma(net(\mathbf{x}_p))\; x_{p,j} = -2\sum_{p=1}^{l}\delta_p\, x_{p,j}.
$$

This derivative answers the question: **how much does a change in $w_j$ affect the change in $E$?**

Gradient descent is identical to the one for the linear unit, with a new delta:

<Formula f={F.sigmoidDelta} />

**In plain words.** The delta is now made of two factors: the error (right answer minus given answer) multiplied by the derivative of the sigmoid at the point where the unit is. If the sigmoid is flat there, the delta is small even when the error is large.

<Figure n="6.11" title="The logistic and its derivatives">
  <SigmoidDerivatives />
  <Caption>The logistic $f_\sigma$ (black), its derivative $f'_\sigma$ (red) and the second derivative $f''_\sigma$ (green). The gray regions are the saturation regions, where $f'_\sigma$ is almost zero. Drag the net input along the axis.</Caption>
</Figure>

It is still an **error-correction** rule, with a few remarks:

- the slope $a$ affects the size of the descent step;
- $f'_\sigma$ is **largest for $net$ close to 0** (almost linear region): there the deltas can be large;
- $f'_\sigma$ is **smallest in the saturated cases**, that is, when $f$ is close to 0 or to 1 and the curve is flat: the deltas are small and the weights change extremely slowly. We must **avoid premature saturation** (too early), for example by **starting with small weights**: this way the initial net is close to zero.

<Callout type="tip" title="A bridge between LMS and the Perceptron">

When a pattern is classified correctly “with confidence” (output saturated close to the target), $f'_\sigma \approx 0$ and the correction is almost zero, just as in the Perceptron, which does not correct the correct patterns. So by using $f_\sigma$ we approximate not only the LTU, but also the Perceptron algorithm.

</Callout>

## Neural networks: the Multi-Layer Perceptron

All the ingredients are ready. An <T id="mlp">**MLP**</T> can be seen in two ways:

- **A.** as a **network of units connected** to each other;
- **B.** as a **flexible function** $h(\mathbf{x})$, made of nested nonlinear functions (one inside the other).

<Figure n="6.12" title="The two views of a feedforward network" size="wide">
  <TwoViews />
  <Caption>The two views of a feedforward network with one hidden layer: network of units (A: inputs $i$, hidden units $j$, output $k$, weights $w_{ji}$ and $w_{kj}$) and flexible function (B: $h(\mathbf{x}) = f_k(\sum_j w_{kj} f_j(\sum_i w_{ji} x_i))$). The color of the nodes is their value; hover over a hidden unit or a row to link the two views.</Caption>
</Figure>

### View A: a network of units

In an MLP architecture:

- the units are joined by weighted connections and organized in **layers**, that is, in successive rows;
- the **input layer** is only the source of the input $\mathbf{x}$: it loads (copies) the pattern, computing neither $net$ nor $f$;
- the **hidden layer** sends its outputs to the output layer (or to another hidden layer). It is called “hidden” because it cannot be seen from outside: it is neither input nor answer;
- the **output layer** produces $h(\mathbf{x})$, the answer of the network.

### View B: a flexible function

A two-layer network (one hidden and one output layer) computes

<Formula f={F.mlp} />

**In plain words.** It is read from the inside out. First every hidden unit $j$ takes the weighted sum of the inputs and passes it through its function $f_j$. Then the output unit $k$ takes the weighted sum of the outputs of the hidden units and passes it through its function $f_k$. It is the same operation as the artificial neuron, repeated twice in a row.

Here $x_i$ are the input variables, $w$ the free parameters (collected in weight [matrices](#/prerequisiti/matrici)) and $f$ nonlinear functions (sigmoids). Compare with the linear function $h(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_i w_i x_i$: the network is a composition of linear functions (the weighted sums) and nonlinear ones (the sigmoids).

### Components of a neural network

A network is traditionally described by three things:

- the type of **unit** (how it computes the net and which activation function it uses);
- the **architecture** (number of units, topology, that is, how they are connected, number of layers);
- the **learning algorithm**.

### Uniform notation for the units

It is convenient to have a single way of writing that holds for all the units. For a generic unit $t$ (which can be hidden, $j$, or output, $k$):

$$
net_t(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_u w_{tu}\, o_u, \qquad o_t(\mathbf{x}) = f_t\big(net_t(\mathbf{x})\big),
$$

where $u$ denotes a generic input source (which can be $i$ or $j$). Since the input layer loads the pattern, we can use the symbol $o$ both for the inputs and for the outputs of the hidden units. So the input that reaches unit $t$ from source $u$ (through the connection $w_{tu}$) is always called $o_u$. This notation will be used in the derivation of backpropagation.

<Callout type="example" title="Exercise (from the lecture)">

1. For the weights $w_{ji}$ (input → hidden), the input $o_u$ is $o_i = x_i$, the component of the pattern.
2. For the weights $w_{kj}$ (hidden → output), the input $o_u$ is $o_j$, the output of hidden unit $j$.
3. $w_{t0}$ is the **bias** of each unit $t$, with constant input $o_0 = 1$.
4. Hidden unit: $o_j = f_j\left(\sum_i w_{ji}\,x_i\right)$; output unit: $o_k = f_k\left(\sum_j w_{kj}\,o_j\right)$.

</Callout>

### Architecture and feedforward processing

The **architecture** defines the topology of the connections, that is, who is connected to whom. The two-layer network described above is the standard MLP, **fully connected**: every unit of a layer is connected to all the units of the next layer. We can have more hidden layers and also connections that “skip” layers.

<Figure n="6.13" title="MLP architectures and feedforward processing" size="wide">
  <Architectures />
  <Caption>MLP architectures: two fully connected layers (with one hidden layer, yellow border) and three layers with other connections (dashed, they skip a layer). Press the buttons to follow the feedforward processing of a pattern, layer after layer.</Caption>
</Figure>

The <T id="feedforward">**feedforward**</T> processing of a pattern goes from the input to the output, never going back:

1. the pattern $\mathbf{x}$ is loaded into the input layer;
2. the outputs of all the units of the first hidden layer are computed;
3. then those of the second hidden layer, and so on;
4. the outputs of the output layer are computed, that is, $h(\mathbf{x})$;
5. the error (delta) at the output can now be computed.

<Callout type="note" title="Recurrent networks">

**Feedforward** networks have a single direction, input → output. **Recurrent networks** add feedback connections (cycles: an output goes back as an input). Self-loops (a unit connected to itself) give the network dynamic properties and a **memory** of past computations. In this way the model extends to the processing of **sequences** (and structured data). They will be covered in a dedicated lecture.

</Callout>

## Why neural networks are flexible

Three questions guide the analysis: why are networks a flexible model (and why can they be seen as an LBE, that is, a *linear basis expansion*)? Does the flexibility have a theoretical foundation? How are the weights learned?

### Which tasks

The hypothesis space is the **continuous space of all the functions** that can be obtained by giving values to the weights of a fixed architecture. Depending on the output units, the network handles:

- **classification**, with a sigmoidal output;
- **regression**, with a linear output.

With **several output units** we get multiple regression (several numbers as the answer) or multi-class classifiers (for example three outputs that give $0.2;\ 0.7;\ 0.1$ for three classes: the second one wins).

<Figure n="6.14" title="A network with multiple outputs" size="wide">
  <MultiOutput />
  <Caption>A network with multiple outputs for a multi-class problem: two inputs, a hidden layer of three units and three output units, which for the initial input give $0.2;\ 0.7;\ 0.1$. Change the input and see which class wins.</Caption>
</Figure>

### The network as an adaptive basis expansion

The network computes $h(\mathbf{x}) = f_k\left(\sum_j w_{kj}\,f_j\left(\sum_i w_{ji}x_i\right)\right)$. Units and architecture are only a graphical way of drawing the flow of the data. Each term

$$
\phi_j(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{w}) = f_j\left(\sum_i w_{ji}\,x_i\right)
$$

can be seen in two ways: as the computation of an independent hidden unit, or as a **basis function $\phi$ of an LBE** (one of the “transformed features” seen in lecture 5). Let us compare:

- **LBE**: $h(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_j w_j\,\phi_j(\mathbf{x})$ (regression) or $h(\mathbf{x}) = f\left(\sum_j w_j\,\phi_j(\mathbf{x})\right)$ (classification). The $\phi$ are **fixed a priori**, that is, chosen before seeing the data, and the model is linear in the parameters.
- **Neural network**: $h(\mathbf{x}) = f_k\left(\sum_j w_{kj}\,\phi_j(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{w})\right)$. Now **the $\phi$ depend on the weights** and therefore **adapt to the data** during training.

<Callout type="tip" title="Neural networks as adaptive bases">

Neural networks are an **adaptive basis function** approach: the bases themselves are adapted to the data (by learning the $\mathbf{w}$ that sit inside the $\phi$). All the bases have the same type (given by the activation function). $h(\mathbf{x})$ is a nonlinear function of weighted sums of nonlinearly transformed linear models, with the decisive improvement of **adaptivity**. (To be compared later with SVMs, another form of LBE.)

</Callout>

Every hidden unit computes a new **nonlinear derived feature**, adaptively: a new property of the data, built starting from the inputs. In other words: the representational capacity of the model depends on the presence of a **hidden layer with nonlinear activations**, which transforms the input into the **internal representation** of the network. Learning builds a suitable internal representation, that is, new hidden features of the data. These allow the model to extract the **higher-order statistics** (complex relations among several inputs together) that are needed to approximate the target function.

<Callout type="warning" title="Hidden units must be nonlinear">

An MLP with **linear** units is equivalent to a **single-layer** network: the composition of linear functions is still linear. With $f$ the identity, $h(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_j w_{kj}\sum_i w_{ji}x_i = \sum_i\left(\sum_j w_{kj}w_{ji}\right)x_i = \sum_i w'_i x_i$. In the calculation the terms were only regrouped: every $x_i$ ends up multiplied by a single number $w'_i$. The hidden layer added nothing.

The price of nonlinearity: the model is **nonlinear in the parameters** $\mathbf{w}$, and training becomes a **nonlinear optimization** problem (non-convex: the error surface is no longer a bowl with a single bottom).

</Callout>

### Universal approximation

<Callout type="theorem" title="Universal approximation theorem (Cybenko 1989, Hornik et al.)">

A network with **a single hidden layer** (with logistic activations) and a linear output can approximate as well as we like **every continuous function** (on hypercubes, that is, on “boxes” of the input space), provided there are enough hidden units. In formulas: given $f$ and $\varepsilon > 0$ (a maximum error chosen at will, as small as we like), there exists $h$ such that $|f(\mathbf{x}) - h(\mathbf{x})| < \varepsilon$ for every $\mathbf{x}$ in the hypercube. More generally, an MLP can approximate as well as we like every input-output mapping, with enough hidden units (<T id="approssimazione-universale">universal approximation</T>).

</Callout>

(It can be thought of as a generalization of approximation with finite Fourier series.)

<Figure n="6.15" title="More hidden units, better approximation">
  <UniversalApprox />
  <Caption>An intuitive construction (not Cybenko’s proof), in one dimension: each logistic hidden unit with a large slope is a soft step centered at $c_j$, and the linear output sums their jumps; with enough units the network (red) follows the continuous function (green) with an error as small as desired. Choose the number of units and the slope.</Caption>
</Figure>

<Deep title="Why a sum of steps approximates every continuous function" kind="intuizione">

Divide $[0, 1]$ into $N$ intervals of width $1/N$. A continuous function on a closed interval is uniformly continuous: with $N$ large enough, inside every interval it varies by less than $\varepsilon$. Then the “staircase” function that equals $f$ at the division points is less than $\varepsilon$ away from $f$. And a staircase is exactly a sum of steps, that is, of very steep sigmoids, each weighted by the jump $f(t_{j+1}) - f(t_j)$. This is the idea of the figure: how to build a curve out of many small stairs. The actual theorem guarantees more (also in several dimensions and with learned weights), but it does not say how many units are needed nor how to find them.

</Deep>

<Callout type="warning" title="It is an existence theorem">

The theorem says that the network **exists**, but it provides neither the **algorithm** to find it nor the **number of units** needed.

</Callout>

So two fundamental questions remain: **how to learn** (backpropagation, next lecture) and **how to choose the architecture**.

### Expressive power and VC-dimension

The expressive power of a network (how many different functions it can represent) depends on the **number of units** and on their **configuration**. The number of units is related to the <T id="vc-dimension">VC-dimension</T> (the measure of the complexity of a model seen in lecture 4). The capabilities of the network depend on the number of parameters (in a fully connected MLP, roughly the number of inputs times the number of hidden units, plus the hidden units times the outputs), but also on the **value** of the weights:

- weights $= 0$ → minimum VC-dim;
- small weights → we work in the linear part of the sigmoid (close to zero, where it is almost a straight line) → small VC-dim;
- large weights → more complex model.

Training starts from weights close to 0 and the weights grow during training. So the network is a **hypothesis space of variable size**: the effective VC-dim grows during learning. (This will be the basis of *early stopping*.)

### How many layers? (a preview)

The universal approximation theorem says that one hidden layer is enough, but it does not guarantee that a **small** number of units is enough. There are results of the “*no flattening*” type, which concern efficiency, not expressiveness: there are functions that with a single hidden layer would require an **exponential** number of units (with respect to the input dimension), while with more layers far fewer are enough. But is it easy to train an MLP with many layers? We will see that in deep learning.

### The inductive bias of neural networks

For networks trained with backpropagation, the <T id="bias-induttivo">inductive bias</T> (the underlying assumption that the model makes about the data) is related to the **smoothness** properties of the functions: small changes in the input produce small changes in the output (for example, a locally bounded first derivative). It is a very common assumption in ML, and it is reasonable: if the target function is not smooth (for example a random number generator), generalization is impossible anyway.

## Toward the learning algorithm

The learning algorithm modifies the weights of the network to get the best approximation of the target function. As always, it does so by minimizing an error function (or loss) on the training data.

### The credit assignment problem

- The Perceptron has a learning algorithm, but it does not represent all Boolean functions.
- A network of perceptrons represents every Boolean function, but an algorithm is needed to train it.

What changes? The **<T id="credit-assignment">credit assignment</T> problem** (assigning the merit, or the blame): how much “responsibility” for the error should be given to the hidden units? For the output units we know the desired answer and therefore the error. For the hidden units **we do not know what their output should be**, so the error signal cannot be measured directly. It is like a team that loses: the final result is visible, but understanding how much each single player got wrong is harder. In addition, the model is nonlinear in the weights.

Minsky and Papert (1969) considered the problem too hard. It was solved by several researchers with the **backpropagation** algorithm, made popular by Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams in the *PDP* book (1986): it was the **renaissance** of neural networks.

### The loading problem

<Callout type="definition" title="Loading problem">

Given a network and a set of examples, is there a set of weights for which the network is consistent with the examples, that is, answers correctly on all of them? (“Loading” the training data into the free parameters of the network: <T id="loading-problem">loading problem</T>.)

</Callout>

The loading problem is **NP-complete** (Judd, 1990): it belongs to a family of problems for which no fast (polynomial) algorithm is known. In practice, however, networks are trained in reasonable time with backpropagation, even though finding the best solution is not guaranteed.

### The key idea: extending gradient descent

Gradient descent that minimizes a loss **extends to MLPs**, provided that the loss and the activation functions are **differentiable**: the point is to find the delta for **every** unit of the network. The problem stays the same: given $l$ examples $(\mathbf{x}_p, d_p)$ and a loss $L$ (for example $L(h(\mathbf{x}_p), d_p) = (d_p - h_\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x}_p))^2$), find the weights $\mathbf{w}$ that minimize

$$
E(\mathbf{w}) = R_{emp} = \frac{1}{l}\sum_{p=1}^{l}\big(d_p - h(\mathbf{x}_p)\big)^2
$$

by computing its gradient. It is the mean of the squared errors over all the examples. Three things are needed: a differentiable loss, differentiable activations and a network along which to follow the flow of information.

Properties of <T id="backpropagation">backpropagation</T>:

- it is simple thanks to the **compositional form** of the model (functions inside functions);
- it uses only quantities that are **local** to each unit (what the unit has nearby), preserving its modularity;
- it is **efficient**: it costs $O(\#W)$ instead of $O(\#W^2)$, that is, a number of operations proportional to the number of weights $\#W$ and not to its square, thanks to the factorization of the deltas;
- on biological plausibility the debate is open (it has been hypothesized that the brain uses a suboptimal local approximation of BP).

The full derivation is in the next lecture (and in the notes [on backpropagation](#/lezione/07)).

### Practical issues in training

The model is often **over-parameterized** (it has more parameters than would be needed) and the optimization is **non-convex** and potentially unstable. The issues to be faced (after backpropagation) are:

- initial values of the weights, learning rate, on-line/batch;
- overfitting and regularization;
- input scaling and output representation;
- number of hidden units;
- multiple minima;
- stopping criteria.

<Callout type="abstract" title="Summary">

The artificial neuron computes $o = f(\sum_j w_j x_j)$: weighted sum and then activation function. The **Perceptron** (threshold) has a learning algorithm that **converges in a finite number of steps** for linearly separable problems, but it does not represent XOR. Two-layer networks of perceptrons represent every Boolean function thanks to the **internal re-representation** of the hidden layer. By replacing the threshold with a differentiable **sigmoid** we can use gradient descent. The **MLP** is a nested function $h(\mathbf{x}) = f_k(\sum_j w_{kj} f_j(\sum_i w_{ji} x_i))$, which can be read as an **adaptive basis expansion**, and it is a **universal approximator**. To train it we must solve the **credit assignment** of the hidden units: backpropagation does it.

</Callout>

<Q q="Describe the artificial neuron and the main activation functions.">

Unit $i$ computes the net input $net_i = \sum_j w_{ij}x_j$, that is, the weighted sum of the inputs (the bias $w_{i0}$ enters with $x_0 = 1$; $w_{ij}$ is the weight from $j$ to $i$), and then the output $o_i = f(net_i)$. Activations: linear (identity), threshold/step (Perceptron, LTU), logistic $1/(1+e^{-ax})$ in $[0,1]$ and tanh in $[-1,1]$ (smooth and differentiable, with slope $a$); also RBF, softmax, stochastic neurons, ReLU $\max(0,x)$ and softplus.

</Q>
<Q q="How are AND, OR, NOT represented with a perceptron? How is XOR solved?">

With output 1 if $net > 0$: AND with $w_1 = w_2 = 1$, $w_0 = -1.5$; OR with $w_1 = w_2 = 1$, $w_0 = -0.5$; NOT with $w_1 = -1$, $w_0 = 0.5$. XOR is not linearly separable, but $x_1 \oplus x_2 = \bar h_1 \wedge h_2$ with $h_1$ = AND and $h_2$ = OR (De Morgan). So we use a two-layer network: two hidden units (AND, OR) and an output with weights $-1$, $+1$ and bias $-0.5$. In the space $(h_1, h_2)$ the two positives end up at the same point $(0,1)$ and the problem becomes separable.

</Q>
<Q q="Write the Perceptron learning algorithm.">

Initial weights zero or small, $\eta \in (0,1)$. As long as the weights change, for every pattern $(\mathbf{x}, d)$ with $d = \pm1$: compute $out = \operatorname{sign}(\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x})$; if $out = d$ do nothing; otherwise $\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} + \eta d\mathbf{x}$ (equivalent to $\mathbf{w} + \tfrac12\eta(d - out)\mathbf{x}$). It is on-line and minimizes the number of errors.

</Q>
<Q q="State and prove the Perceptron convergence theorem.">

If the problem is linearly separable the algorithm converges in a finite number of steps. Proof: flip the negatives ($\mathbf{x}'_i = d_i\mathbf{x}_i$) so that all the targets are positive; with $\mathbf{w}(0) = 0$, $\eta = 1$, after $q$ errors $\mathbf{w}(q) = \sum_j \mathbf{x}_{i_j}$. Lower bound: $\mathbf{w}^*\mathbf{w}(q) \ge q\alpha$ and by Cauchy-Schwarz $\|\mathbf{w}(q)\|^2 \ge (q\alpha)^2/\|\mathbf{w}^*\|^2$. Upper bound: $\|\mathbf{w}(q)\|^2 \le \|\mathbf{w}(q-1)\|^2 + \|\mathbf{x}_{i_q}\|^2$ because $\mathbf{w}(q-1)\mathbf{x}_{i_q} \le 0$, hence $\le q\beta$. Together: $q \le \beta\|\mathbf{w}^*\|^2/\alpha^2$, that is, the errors are at most a fixed number.

</Q>
<Q q="Differences between the Perceptron algorithm and LMS; why can LMS fail on separable problems?">

The Perceptron uses $\delta = d - \operatorname{sign}(\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x})$, corrects only the errors, converges in a finite number of steps if the problem is separable, does not converge otherwise and is hard to extend to networks. LMS uses $\delta = d - \mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x}$ (no threshold), minimizes the squared error with asymptotic convergence even on non-separable problems and can be extended to networks with the gradient. Precisely because it minimizes the squared error, it modifies the weights even for patterns that are already correct: a pattern that is correct but far from the hyperplane has a large squared error and moves the line, which can then get other patterns wrong (figure 6.9).

</Q>
<Q q="Derive the update rule for a single sigmoidal unit.">

$E = \sum_p (d_p - f_\sigma(\mathbf{x}_p^T\mathbf{w}))^2$. By the chain rule (error → output → net → weight) $\frac{\partial E_p}{\partial w_j} = \frac{\partial E_p}{\partial o_p}\frac{\partial o_p}{\partial net_p}\frac{\partial net_p}{\partial w_j} = -2(d_p - o_p)f'_\sigma(net_p)x_{p,j}$. Hence $\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} + \eta\delta_p\mathbf{x}_p$ with $\delta_p = (d_p - o_p)f'_\sigma(net_p)$ and $f'_\sigma = f_\sigma(1 - f_\sigma)$: the correction is large close to $net = 0$ and almost zero in saturation (it is better to start from small weights).

</Q>
<Q q="Why must the hidden units be nonlinear?">

Because a composition of linear functions is still linear: with $f$ the identity $h(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_j w_{kj}\sum_i w_{ji}x_i = \sum_i w'_i x_i$, that is, a single-layer network. The representational capacity comes from the nonlinear hidden layer; the price is that the model becomes nonlinear in the weights and the optimization non-convex.

</Q>
<Q q="In what sense is a neural network an adaptive LBE?">

Every hidden unit computes $\phi_j(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{w}) = f_j(\sum_i w_{ji}x_i)$, which is a basis function; the output is $f_k(\sum_j w_{kj}\phi_j)$. In an LBE the $\phi$ are fixed before seeing the data and the model is linear in the parameters. In the network the $\phi$ depend on the weights and adapt to the data during training (learned derived features, internal representation).

</Q>
<Q q="State the universal approximation theorem and discuss its limits.">

A network with a single logistic hidden layer and a linear output approximates as well as we like every continuous function on a hypercube, with enough hidden units: for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $h$ with $|f - h| < \varepsilon$. Limits: it is an existence theorem, it gives neither the algorithm to find the weights nor the number of units needed (which can be exponential: “no flattening” results in favor of more layers).

</Q>
<Q q="What is the credit assignment problem? And the loading problem?">

Credit assignment: establishing how much responsibility for the error to give to the hidden units, whose desired output is not known (the error signal cannot be measured directly); backpropagation solves it. Loading problem: deciding whether there exist weights with which a network is consistent with a set of examples; it is NP-complete (Judd, 1990), but in practice backpropagation trains networks in reasonable time, with no guarantee of finding the optimum.

</Q>
