## What Machine Learning is

### Learning as a universal principle

Learning is a principle shared by living beings, societies and — today — machines. As Poggio and Shelton observe (*AI Magazine*, 1999), *the problem of learning is arguably at the very core of the problem of intelligence, both biological and artificial*. Understanding how learning happens therefore means understanding a fundamental part of what it means to be intelligent.

<Callout type="definition" title="Machine Learning">

**Machine Learning** is a theoretical and applied area of computer science that combines two goals:

- that of artificial intelligence, namely building **computers that are able to learn**;
- having **powerful adaptive and statistical tools**, with rigorous foundations in the computational sciences.

Operationally, it is the automatic learning, by a system, from **experience** (a set of examples), aimed at solving a computational task.

</Callout>

<Deep title="The three keywords of the definition" kind="intuizione">

The operational definition has three ingredients, which we will meet again in every lesson:

- a **system** that learns (the model);
- the **experience**, that is, a set of examples (the data);
- a **computational task** to be solved (the task).

One does not write the solution: one writes a system that is able to derive it from the examples.

</Deep>

### Why make machines learn: luxury or necessity?

ML is not a technological whim but a **necessity**, for two main reasons.

1. **Growing availability of empirical data** and the need to analyze them. Science itself is shifting paradigm toward a *data-driven* approach, in which knowledge is extracted from data; ML plays a central, methodological role in this change.
2. **It is hard to program adaptivity and intelligence explicitly.** Alan Turing had already sensed this: for many tasks writing the rules by hand is impractical, and learning becomes the only way to endow systems with intelligent behavior.

The combination of **large amounts of data**, **computing power** (HPC, GPUs) and the **flexibility of ML models** has opened a new era of artificial intelligence.

### When ML is needed

ML is the right choice when for a problem there is **little or no prior knowledge** — we do not know how to write the rules of the solution — but it is relatively **easy to obtain experience**, that is, data for which we know the correct result. Classic examples are the classification of emails as spam, and the recognition of characters, faces or speech.

<Callout type="example" title="Handwritten digit recognition">

Each digit is an image of $8 \times 8$ pixels. Writing by hand the rules that tell a “3” from an “8” for every possible handwriting is practically impossible. Instead, one collects many examples of already labeled digits and lets the system **learn a classifier** $f$ that maps each image to the correct class ($0, 1, \dots, 9$).

</Callout>

### Application areas

ML is by now pervasive in real systems — from OCR to human-machine interfaces, to search engines — and it is the core of an interdisciplinary area that since the 1980s has included:

- **Pattern Recognition** (face and speech recognition), **Computer Vision**, **Natural Language Processing** (from text classification to speech processing, up to generative AI);
- **Robotics**, adaptive systems and filters, intelligent sensor networks, personalized components;
- **Knowledge Discovery and Data Mining**, Information Retrieval, analysis of complex data (medicine, biology, chemistry, web, marketing), financial forecasting.

In some fields — NLP, Pattern Recognition, Computer Vision — the impact has been revolutionary, to the point that people speak of a “new AI”.

### The ultimate goal

Anything powerful can be dangerous if misused; but the ultimate goal of AI and ML is to **bring benefits to people** by solving big and small problems, to **accelerate human progress** and to provide intelligence to every other scientific field: to augment our abilities, enhancing our humanity (P. Domingos, *Scientific American*, 2018).

## Why study Machine Learning

Within the Master’s degree programme, ML serves to:

- know the **basic principles of learning processes**, from the computational point of view;
- know **new computing paradigms**, such as **neural networks**: studied as a computational paradigm since the 1940s with a neurobiological inspiration, today they are a set of powerful models for **function approximation** with predictive capabilities, supported by a solid theory (*learning theory*), and they are the basis of **Deep Learning**;
- be able to **apply these models rigorously**;
- have the foundations for all the subsequent AI courses.

### Modern ML

ML has gone from being a **collection of heuristic methods** — tricks coming from biology, statistics, neural networks, fuzzy logic… — to the construction of **general conceptual frameworks**. A new understanding has emerged of the principles that govern learning processes, with three important consequences:

- expressing different models in different mathematical or computer science “languages” **does not change the principles**;
- different application fields **do not change the principles**;
- new technological advances **do not change the principles**.

<Callout type="tip" title="The common thread of the course">

“Machine Learning” in the proper sense is the **core of principles and methods** for building flexible and powerful computational models from data. Individual models change, the principles remain: this is what is really worth learning.

</Callout>

### Three points of view on ML

<Cards>
  <Card kicker="1" title="AI methodology">To build **adaptive and intelligent systems**.</Card>
  <Card kicker="2" title="Statistical learning">Inference of hypotheses according to mathematical principles, to build powerful **predictive systems** for intelligent data analysis.</Card>
  <Card kicker="3" title="Computer science method for innovative areas">Using the models as **tools** for complex and interdisciplinary problems.</Card>
</Cards>

The course considers all of them, with a **critical approach**: for the various methods, limits and appropriateness of use are discussed, with attention to the underlying principles. There are two practical goals: learning to **develop new ML models** and learning to **apply state-of-the-art methodologies** to problems from other fields.

## Course information

### Prerequisites and objectives

No course is strictly required: we start from scratch. What is needed, however, is the typical background of a scientific Bachelor’s degree: **calculus** (functions, differential calculus), **matrix notation and calculus**, **elements of probability and statistics**, **algorithms**.

The course introduces the principles and the critical analysis of the main paradigms — models and algorithms, with a focus on neural networks — for learning from data. The concepts come progressively, from the simplest approaches up to state-of-the-art models, within the conceptual framework of modern ML: the **learning of functions from examples**. The focus is on:

- critical analysis of the characteristics for the design and use of ML;
- **rigorous experimental evaluation**: there is a big difference between *using* ML tools and using them *correctly*;
- computational aspects of learning systems.

### Structure of the course

The course has a strong structure: it starts from simple models and arrives at the state of the art, and the fundamental concepts are introduced through different models. The practical advice is twofold: (1) study the individual methods, (2) look for the **connections and comparisons** among them, so as to abstract the concepts. The common thread is understanding the relationship between **model flexibility** and **complexity control**, a fundamental concept for the capacity of <T id="generalizzazione">generalization</T>.

## Mathematical background

A few basic concepts are needed to follow the course: multivariable calculus (partial derivatives, gradient), probability (density, mean, variance, normal distribution, conditional and joint probabilities), matrix calculus (dot product, inverse, norms). Topics such as the linear least squares problem with the SVD and Lagrange multipliers can be studied in more depth in parallel. Recommended references: appendix A of AIMA, chapters 2–4 of the *Deep Learning book*, and *Mathematics for Machine Learning* (2020).

The notation used is: $x$ scalar, $\mathbf{x}$ vector (in bold), $X$ matrix (uppercase).

### Dot product and norms

<Callout type="definition" title="Dot product (inner / dot product)">

<Formula f={F.dot} />

Equivalent notations: $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{a}^t\mathbf{b} = \langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}\rangle$, and also just $\mathbf{ab}$ if the context is clear.

</Callout>

The relation with the cosine gives the geometric intuition: the <T id="prodotto-scalare">dot product</T> measures **how much two vectors point in the same direction**. It is maximal when they are parallel, zero when they are orthogonal, negative when they point in opposite directions. This reading will come back again and again: for example in linear models, where $\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x}$ measures the “alignment” of the input with the weights.

<Deep title="Worked example: two vectors in ℝ²" kind="esempio">

With $\mathbf{a} = (3, 1)$ and $\mathbf{b} = (1, 2)$: $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = 3 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 2 = 5$. The lengths are $|\mathbf{a}| = \sqrt{10} \approx 3.16$ and $|\mathbf{b}| = \sqrt{5} \approx 2.24$, hence $\cos\theta = 5 / \sqrt{50} \approx 0.71$: the angle is about $45^\circ$. The product is positive, the vectors point “the same way”.

</Deep>

### Partial derivatives and gradient

The **partial derivative** generalizes the derivative to functions of several variables: it is the derivative with respect to a single variable while keeping all the others fixed. For $f(x_1, x_2, x_3)$ one computes $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}$, $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2}$, $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_3}$.

<Callout type="definition" title="Gradient">

The <T id="gradiente">gradient</T> of $f$ is the vector of its partial derivatives:

<Formula f={F.gradient} />

where the $\mathbf{e}_i$ are the orthogonal unit vectors along the coordinate directions.

</Callout>

<Deep title="Worked example: computing a gradient" kind="esempio">

Let $f(x_1, x_2) = x_1^2 + 3x_1x_2$. Differentiating with respect to $x_1$ (with $x_2$ fixed, as if it were a constant) gives $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1} = 2x_1 + 3x_2$; differentiating with respect to $x_2$ (with $x_1$ fixed) gives $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2} = 3x_1$. Hence $\nabla f = (2x_1 + 3x_2,\; 3x_1)$ and, for example, at the point $(1, 2)$ we have $\nabla f(1,2) = (8, 3)$.

</Deep>

The geometric meaning is fundamental for the whole course: at a point, the gradient is a vector that **points in the direction of steepest slope**, and its norm tells **how steep** the slope is. The gradient therefore indicates where the function increases most rapidly, while its opposite $-\nabla f$ indicates the direction in which it **decreases** most rapidly. This is exactly the idea behind <T id="discesa-del-gradiente">gradient descent</T>, which we will use to train models by minimizing an error function.

A point where the gradient is zero is called a <T id="punto-stazionario">stationary point</T>. It can be a local minimum, a local maximum or a saddle point: the zero gradient alone is not enough to tell which case it is.

## Summary and next steps

<Callout type="abstract" title="Summary">

ML is the automatic learning of a task from examples: it is necessary when data abound but explicit rules are missing. The course approaches it as **function approximation from examples**, building the models through successive “building blocks”, with attention to the principles (which do not change as the models vary) and to rigorous evaluation. The key concept that runs through the whole course is the trade-off between **model flexibility** and **complexity control**, on which **generalization** depends.

</Callout>

The following lectures complete the introduction: [lessons 2–3](#/lezione/03) cover the usefulness of ML, learning as function approximation with a running example, and the design components of an ML system (task, hypothesis space, inductive bias, loss function, generalization); [lesson 4](#/lezione/04) goes deeper into generalization and validation.

<Exam>
<Q q="Why is ML a necessity and not a luxury? Under which conditions is it appropriate to use it?">

For two reasons: the **growing availability of empirical data** to be analyzed (science itself becomes *data-driven*) and the **difficulty of explicitly programming** adaptivity and intelligence — for many tasks we do not know how to write the rules. It is appropriate when there is **little or no prior knowledge** about the solution but it is **easy to obtain examples** with the correct result (spam, recognition of characters, faces, speech).

</Q>
<Q q="What is the gradient and why does $-\nabla f$ indicate the direction of steepest decrease?">

It is the vector of the partial derivatives, $\nabla f = (\partial f/\partial x_1, \dots, \partial f/\partial x_n)$. At every point it points in the direction of **steepest slope** and its norm tells how steep it is. For a small step $\Delta\mathbf{x}$ the change in $f$ is approximately $\nabla f \cdot \Delta\mathbf{x}$: for a given step length it is largest if $\Delta\mathbf{x}$ is parallel to $\nabla f$ and smallest (the most negative) if it is opposite, that is, along $-\nabla f$. This is the idea of gradient descent.

</Q>
<Q q="What is a stationary point? Is it always a minimum?">

It is a point where the gradient is zero. It is **not** always a minimum: it can be a local minimum, a local maximum or a saddle point. The zero gradient alone is not enough to distinguish the three cases (this is clearly visible in figure 1.5, comparing “Bowl”, “Dome” and “Saddle”).

</Q>
</Exam>
