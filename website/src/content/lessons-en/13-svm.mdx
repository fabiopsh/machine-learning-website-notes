<Lead>**Support Vector Machines** (SVMs) are the answer to the question left open by SLT ([lesson 12](#/lezione/12)): is it possible to fix the training error and minimize the VC-confidence **automatically**? The SVM is still a linear machine (an LTU), but among all the separating hyperplanes it chooses the one that **maximizes the margin**, and this amounts to controlling the VC-dimension. The lesson proceeds in four steps: linear hard margin SVM, soft margin SVM for non-separable data, **kernels** for nonlinear problems, and finally SVM for **regression**.</Lead>

<Callout type="note" title="Notation (from Haykin, ch. 6)">

$N$ is the number of examples (previously $l$), with indices $i$ and $j$ for the patterns; $m$ is the dimension of the input vector (previously $n$); $b$ is used instead of $w_0$ for the bias. The parts marked with * in the slides are useful for completeness but not essential for using the model (and for a standard oral exam): in particular the details of the solution with Lagrange multipliers.

</Callout>

## Part I — SVM for binary classification

An <T id="svm">SVM</T> is:

- a **linear machine** (like the LTU or the Perceptron);
- that **maximizes the margin of separation**;
- and implements a **Structural Risk Minimization**.

Initially (hard margin SVM) the problem is assumed to be **linearly separable** and the data to contain **no errors**.

<Figure n="13.1" title="Separable and non-separable" size="wide">
  <Separable />
  <Caption>Linearly separable patterns (left) and non-linearly separable patterns (right): on the right the points of one class are enclosed in a circle by those of the other. Drag the handles of the two lines and count the errors.</Caption>
</Figure>

### The separating hyperplane

Given the training set $T = \{(\mathbf{x}_i, d_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$, we look for a hyperplane $\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x} + b = 0$ that separates the examples:

$$
\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x}_i + b \ge 0 \;\text{ if } d_i = +1, \qquad \mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x}_i + b < 0 \;\text{ if } d_i = -1.
$$

$g(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x} + b$ is the **discriminant function** and $h(\mathbf{x}) = \operatorname{sign}(g(\mathbf{x}))$ is the **hypothesis**.

### The margin of separation

<Callout type="definition" title="Margin of separation">

If the hyperplane is at the same distance from the closest positive example and from the closest negative one, the <T id="margine">**margin of separation**</T> $\rho$ is twice the distance between the hyperplane and the closest point. It can be thought of as a **“safety zone”** around the boundary.

</Callout>

Not all the hyperplanes that solve the problem are equal: as the separating hyperplane varies, the margin varies too.

<Figure n="13.2" title="Different margins, optimal hyperplane" size="wide">
  <MarginExplorer />
  <Caption>The margin of separation $\rho$ (gray zone, bounded by the dashed lines passing through the closest points) and different separating hyperplanes with different margins. Drag the two handles to change the hyperplane; the button shows the maximum-margin one, computed by actually solving the SVM problem.</Caption>
</Figure>

<Callout type="definition" title="Optimal hyperplane">

The <T id="iperpiano-ottimo">**optimal hyperplane**</T> $\mathbf{w}_o^T\mathbf{x} + b_o = 0$ (“o” stands for *optimal*) is the one that **maximizes the margin** $\rho$. We will see that $\rho = \dfrac{2}{\|\mathbf{w}\|}$, hence

$$
\text{maximizing } \rho \iff \text{minimizing } \|\mathbf{w}\|.
$$

</Callout>

### Canonical representation and support vectors

Thanks to the **freedom of scale** (multiplying $\mathbf{w}$ and $b$ by a constant does not change the hyperplane) we can rescale $\mathbf{w}$ and $b$ so that the points closest to the hyperplane satisfy $|g(\mathbf{x}_i)| = |\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x}_i + b| = 1$. Then:

$$
\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x}_i + b \ge 1 \;\text{ if } d_i = +1, \qquad \mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x}_i + b \le -1 \;\text{ if } d_i = -1,
$$

that is, in compact form:

$$
d_i(\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x}_i + b) \ge 1 \qquad \forall i = 1,\dots,N.
$$

<Callout type="definition" title="Support vector">

A <T id="support-vector">**support vector**</T> $\mathbf{x}^{(s)}$ satisfies the constraint **with equality**: $d^{(s)}(\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x}^{(s)} + b) = 1$. The support vectors are the points **closest** to the hyperplane, those lying on the edge of the margin.

</Callout>

<Figure n="13.3" title="The support vectors" size="wide">
  <SupportVectors />
  <Caption>The support vectors (circled) lie on the lines $\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x} + b = \pm 1$, at the edges of the margin; the hyperplane is $\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x} + b = 0$. Click a point to remove it from the training set: the SVM is retrained on the remaining points.</Caption>
</Figure>

### Computing the margin

**Distance of a point from the hyperplane.** Let $r$ be the distance between $\mathbf{x}$ and the optimal hyperplane. Since $\mathbf{w}_o$ is orthogonal to the hyperplane, we can write

$$
\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}_p + r\,\frac{\mathbf{w}_o}{\|\mathbf{w}_o\|},
$$

where $\mathbf{x}_p$ is the projection of $\mathbf{x}$ onto the hyperplane.

<Figure n="13.4" title="Distance of a point from the hyperplane" size="wide">
  <Distance />
  <Caption>Distance $r$ between a point $\mathbf{x}$ and the optimal hyperplane, with the normal vector $\mathbf{w}_o$ and the projection $\mathbf{x}_p$. Drag $\mathbf{x}$ and compare $r$ with $g(\mathbf{x})/\|\mathbf{w}_o\|$.</Caption>
</Figure>

Evaluating $g(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{w}_o^T\mathbf{x} + b_o$:

$$
g(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{w}_o^T\mathbf{x}_p + b_o + r\,\mathbf{w}_o^T\frac{\mathbf{w}_o}{\|\mathbf{w}_o\|} = \underbrace{g(\mathbf{x}_p)}_{=0} + r\,\frac{\|\mathbf{w}_o\|^2}{\|\mathbf{w}_o\|} = r\,\|\mathbf{w}_o\|,
$$

because $\mathbf{x}_p$ lies on the hyperplane. Hence $r = \dfrac{g(\mathbf{x})}{\|\mathbf{w}_o\|}$.

**Margin.** For a positive support vector $g(\mathbf{x}^{(s)}) = 1$, so its distance from the hyperplane is $r = \frac{1}{\|\mathbf{w}_o\|} = \frac{\rho}{2}$. From which:

<Formula f={F.margin} />

The optimal hyperplane maximizes $\rho$, that is, it **minimizes $\|\mathbf{w}\|$**.

### The quadratic optimization problem (hard margin)

The formal derivation requires **constrained optimization** techniques (assumed to be known, for example from the CM course); quadratic programming is considered to be solved by existing packages. What matters here is to understand how the problem is formulated, the relation between support vectors and multipliers $\alpha$, and the final result for $\mathbf{w}_o$.

<Callout type="definition" title="Primal problem — hard margin SVM">

Given $T = \{(\mathbf{x}_i, d_i)\}_{i=1}^N$, find $\mathbf{w}$ and $b$ that minimize $\Psi(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{w}$ (that is, $\min \|\mathbf{w}\|$) subject to the constraints (zero classification errors) $d_i(\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x}_i + b) \ge 1$ for every $i = 1,\dots,N$:

<Formula f={F.primalHard} />

</Callout>

- The objective function is **quadratic and convex** in $\mathbf{w}$.
- The constraints are **linear** in $\mathbf{w}$.
- Solving this problem scales with the dimension of the input space $m$ (the actual cost depends in any case on the solver’s algorithm).

#### Solution with Lagrange multipliers*

We build the **Lagrangian**:

$$
J(\mathbf{w}, b, \boldsymbol{\alpha}) = \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{w} - \sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_i\big(d_i(\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x}_i + b) - 1\big),
$$

with $\alpha_i \ge 0$ the **Lagrange multipliers**, one for each constraint of the primal. $J$ must be **minimized with respect to $\mathbf{w}$ and $b$** and **maximized with respect to $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$**: the solution is a **saddle point** of $J$.

Optimality conditions:

$$
\frac{\partial J}{\partial \mathbf{w}} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \mathbf{w} = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_i d_i\mathbf{x}_i, \qquad \frac{\partial J}{\partial b} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_i d_i = 0.
$$

**Kuhn-Tucker conditions.** At the saddle point we have

$$
\alpha_i\big(d_i(\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x}_i + b) - 1\big) = 0 \qquad \forall i.
$$

Hence:

- if $\alpha_i > 0$, then $d_i(\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x}_i + b) = 1$: $\mathbf{x}_i$ is a **support vector**;
- if $\mathbf{x}_i$ is not a support vector, then $\alpha_i = 0$.

<Callout type="tip" title="The hyperplane depends only on the support vectors">

$\mathbf{w}_o = \sum_{i=1}^{N_s}\alpha_{o,i}\,d_i\,\mathbf{x}_i$: the sum is restricted to the $N_s$ support vectors. **The hyperplane depends only on the support vectors!** All the other points could be removed without changing the solution (try it in figure 13.3).

</Callout>

#### The dual problem*

Substituting the optimality conditions into $J$ gives the **dual problem**, in which only the multipliers appear:

<Callout type="definition" title="Dual problem — hard margin SVM">

Find $\{\alpha_i\}_{i=1}^N$ that maximize

<Formula f={F.dualHard} />

subject to $\alpha_i \ge 0$ for every $i$ and $\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_i d_i = 0$.

</Callout>

The $\alpha$ are found by solving the **quadratic programming** (QP) problem or with more recent and efficient approaches (for example **SMO**, *Sequential Minimal Optimization*). The dual scales with the **number of examples $N$**, less with the dimension of the space. Note that the data appear **only through dot products** $\mathbf{x}_i^T\mathbf{x}_j$: this will be the key to kernels.

#### Obtaining $\mathbf{w}_o$ and $b_o$

1. Solve the dual, obtaining $\{\alpha_{o,i}\}$.
2. Compute $\mathbf{w}_o = \sum_{i=1}^N\alpha_{o,i}d_i\mathbf{x}_i$.
3. Compute $b_o = 1 - \mathbf{w}_o^T\mathbf{x}^{(s)}$ for a positive support vector $\mathbf{x}^{(s)}$, that is, $b_o = 1 - \sum_{i=1}^N\alpha_{o,i}d_i\,\mathbf{x}_i^T\mathbf{x}^{(s)}$.

### How it is used

There is no need to know $\mathbf{w}_o$ explicitly: the multipliers (from the dual) and the bias are enough. The decision surface is

$$
\mathbf{w}_o^T\mathbf{x} + b_o = 0 \iff \sum_{i=1}^N\alpha_{o,i}\,d_i\,\underbrace{\mathbf{x}_i^T\mathbf{x}}_{\text{dot product}} + b_o = 0.
$$

Given a pattern $\mathbf{x}$: (1) compute $g(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^N\alpha_{o,i}d_i\,\mathbf{x}_i^T\mathbf{x} + b_o$; (2) classify with the sign of $g(\mathbf{x})$. The sum can be restricted to the $N_s$ support vectors.

### Why does it improve generalization?

On linearly separable problems we have **fixed the training error** (to zero). Minimizing the norm of $\mathbf{w}$ then amounts to **minimizing the VC-dimension**, and therefore the capacity term (VC-confidence) $\varepsilon$ in

$$
R[h] \le R_{emp}[h] + \varepsilon(VC, N, \delta).
$$

<Callout type="theorem" title="Vapnik’s theorem">

Let $D$ be the diameter of the smallest ball that contains the data $\mathbf{x}_1,\dots,\mathbf{x}_N$. For the class of separating hyperplanes $\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x} + b = 0$ with margin $\rho$, the VC-dimension is bounded by

$$
VC \le \min\left(\left\lceil\frac{D^2}{\rho^2}\right\rceil, m_0\right) + 1.
$$

</Callout>

Since $\frac{D^2}{\rho^2} \propto \text{Radius}^2\,\|\mathbf{w}\|^2$, the VC-dim can be **smaller than $m_0 + 1$** (that of generic hyperplanes) by restricting the search to the “regularized” hyperplanes with maximum margin. **The larger the margin, the smaller the VC-dim**, regardless of the dimension of the space.

### Why it is an elegant approach

For linearly separable data there are many solutions; Vapnik proposes the maximum-margin **optimal separating hyperplane**, which provides:

- a **unique solution** (unlike the iterative Perceptron algorithm) with **zero errors** (unlike LMS) for the binary classifier;
- an **automated SRM**, which minimizes the VC-confidence (by maximizing the margin) directly within the optimization process, **with no hyperparameters** in the separable case;
- the use of **constrained quadratic programming** solvers (instead of gradient descent), with a nice dual form that shows the support vectors and the dot products between patterns;
- a solution concentrated on **“selected”** training data, the support vectors: a strength, because it does not depend on the points far from the boundary; a weakness, because one hopes that the points on the boundary are not the noisiest ones.

But what to do with **noisy** or **non-linearly separable** data?

## Soft margin SVM: allowing errors

If at least one point violates the exact separation condition $d_i(\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x}_i + b) \ge 1$, we move to the <T id="soft-margin">**soft margin**</T>.

<Callout type="tip" title="The advantage of the soft margin">

Allowing points inside the margin makes it possible to have a **wider margin**: we obtain tolerance to noise and a simpler model. (Exercise: find the margin of the figures without allowing points inside it.)

</Callout>

### Slack variables

We introduce non-negative variables $\xi_i \ge 0$, called <T id="variabili-slack">**slack variables**</T>, and the constraints become

$$
d_i(\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x}_i + b) \ge 1 - \xi_i \qquad \forall i.
$$

- $\xi_i = 0$: the point is outside the margin or on its edge;
- $0 < \xi_i \le 1$: the point is inside the margin but correctly classified;
- $\xi_i > 1$: the point is misclassified.

A support vector now satisfies $d_i(\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x}_i + b) = 1 - \xi_i$.

<Figure n="13.5" title="Soft margin and slack variables" size="wide">
  <SoftMargin />
  <Caption>Patterns that violate the margin, without a classification error (inside the margin, on the right side) and with an error (on the wrong side), and their slack variables $\xi_i$: the distance (in violet) from the edge of the margin of their own class, in units of $1/\|\mathbf{w}\|$. The soft margin SVM is actually solved at every change: drag the two points with the handle and change $C$.</Caption>
</Figure>

Warning: **Vapnik’s theorem no longer holds** (the hard margin does not allow points in the margin).

### The soft margin primal problem

<Callout type="definition" title="Primal problem — soft margin SVM">

Find $\mathbf{w}$ and $b$ that minimize $\Psi(\mathbf{w}, \boldsymbol{\xi})$ subject to $d_i(\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x}_i + b) \ge 1 - \xi_i$ and $\xi_i \ge 0$ for every $i$:

<Formula f={F.primalSoft} />

</Callout>

$C$ is a **regularization hyperparameter** (the fully automatic SRM is lost!): it controls the trade-off between minimizing the empirical risk and minimizing the capacity term (VC-confidence).

- **Low $C$**: many training errors allowed → possible **underfitting**.
- **High $C$**: no errors allowed → smaller margin → possible **overfitting**.

(For $C \to \infty$ we recover the hard margin.)

### The soft margin dual*

<Callout type="definition" title="Dual problem — soft margin SVM">

Find $\{\alpha_i\}$ that maximize

$$
Q(\boldsymbol{\alpha}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_i - \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_i\alpha_j d_i d_j\,\mathbf{x}_i^T\mathbf{x}_j
$$

subject to $\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_i d_i = 0$ and $0 \le \alpha_i \le C$ for every $i$.

</Callout>

It is **identical** to the hard margin case, except for the upper constraint $\alpha_i \le C$. The Kuhn-Tucker conditions become

$$
\alpha_i\big(d_i(\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x}_i + b) + \xi_i - 1\big) = 0, \qquad \mu_i\xi_i = 0,
$$

where the $\mu_i$ are the multipliers that enforce $\xi_i \ge 0$. It follows that:

- $0 < \alpha_i < C \Rightarrow \xi_i = 0$: the point is **on the edge** of the margin;
- $\alpha_i = C \Rightarrow \xi_i \ge 0$: the point is **inside** the margin (or on the wrong side).

**Solution**: $\mathbf{w}_o = \sum_i \alpha_{o,i}d_i\mathbf{x}_i$ and $b_o = d_j - \sum_i\alpha_{o,i}d_i\,\mathbf{x}_i^T\mathbf{x}_j$ for a pattern $j$ with $0 < \alpha_j < C$ (or the average over all of these, for numerical stability). The nonzero multipliers correspond to the support vectors. The model is used as before: $g(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_i\alpha_{o,i}d_i\,\mathbf{x}_i^T\mathbf{x} + b_o$, $h(\mathbf{x}) = \operatorname{sign}(g(\mathbf{x}))$.

## Kernels: SVM for nonlinear problems

### Mapping to a high-dimensional space

<Callout type="definition" title="Idea">

Map the data from the input space to a high-dimensional **feature space**, where they become **linearly separable**.

</Callout>

<Callout type="example" title={<>From <Tex>{'\\mathbb{R}^2'}</Tex> to <Tex>{'\\mathbb{R}^3'}</Tex></>}>

With $\Phi: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^3$, $(x_1, x_2)^T \mapsto (x_1^2, \sqrt{2}x_1x_2, x_2^2)^T$ (second-degree monomials), two classes separated by an ellipse in the input space become separable by a **plane** in the feature space.

</Callout>

<Figure n="13.6" title="From the input space to the feature space" size="wide">
  <FeatureMap />
  <Caption>A function $\Phi(\mathbf{x})$ maps the data to a space in which they are linearly separable (recall the LBE of linear models): in the input space an ellipse is needed, in the space of second-degree monomials $(z_1, z_2, z_3) = (x_1^2, \sqrt{2}x_1x_2, x_2^2)$ a plane is enough. Rotate the feature space by dragging it and change the ellipse.</Caption>
</Figure>

The mapping of the example is built on purpose to solve the problem (the features correspond to ellipsoids in the original space). But finding the “best” transformation **is not automatic** (unless prior knowledge is available); general transformations are often used. **The choice of $\Phi$ is the critical point of SVMs.**

### High-dimensional feature spaces

The procedure is: (1) nonlinear mapping of the patterns to a high-dimensional feature space (by **Cover’s theorem**, the patterns are linearly separable there with high probability); (2) search for the optimal hyperplane in the feature space.

But we know that very large feature spaces (expansions in large bases) can be **computationally infeasible** and lead to **overfitting** without controlling the dimension of the space and the complexity of the classifier. The **kernel approach** makes it possible to handle the feature space **implicitly**, in a regularized setting (where it is the **margin** that controls the complexity, not the dimension of the input space).

With $\Phi: \mathbb{R}^{m_0} \to \mathbb{R}^{m_1}$, the training set becomes $\{(\Phi(\mathbf{x}_i), d_i)\}$ and the hyperplane $\mathbf{w}^T\Phi(\mathbf{x}) + b = 0$. Including the bias ($w_0 = b$, $\phi_0(\mathbf{x}) = 1$), the decision surface $\mathbf{w}^T\Phi(\mathbf{x}) = 0$ is a **linear basis expansion** in which the dimension of the space can be very large and the complexity depends on the **margin**, not on the dimension.

The weight vector is $\mathbf{w} = \sum_i \alpha_i d_i\Phi(\mathbf{x}_i)$, and the hyperplane becomes

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_i d_i\,\underbrace{\Phi^T(\mathbf{x}_i)\Phi(\mathbf{x})}_{\text{dot product}} = 0.
$$

The dot product is now in the feature space, and computing $\Phi(\mathbf{x})$ could be intractable.

### The kernel trick

Under certain conditions **there is no need to compute $\Phi(\mathbf{x})$**, nor even to know the feature space! All we need is a function that directly computes the dot product in the feature space:

<Callout type="definition" title="Kernel (inner product kernel)">

$$
k: \mathbb{R}^{m_0} \times \mathbb{R}^{m_0} \to \mathbb{R}, \qquad k(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}) = \Phi^T(\mathbf{x}_i)\,\Phi(\mathbf{x}).
$$

Property: it is **symmetric**, $k(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}) = k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}_i)$. This is the <T id="kernel">kernel</T>, and using it in place of the mapping is the <T id="kernel-trick">*kernel trick*</T>.

</Callout>

<Callout type="example" title="The kernel of the example">

With $\Phi(\mathbf{x}) = (x_1^2, \sqrt{2}x_1x_2, x_2^2)^T$, for $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, x_2)^T$ and $\mathbf{y} = (y_1, y_2)^T$:

$$
\Phi^T(\mathbf{x})\Phi(\mathbf{y}) = x_1^2y_1^2 + 2x_1x_2y_1y_2 + x_2^2y_2^2 = (x_1y_1 + x_2y_2)^2 = (\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{y})^2 = k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}).
$$

**Very important**: the dot product in the feature space (3D) is computed **without going through the mapping**, directly in terms of the input patterns (2D).

</Callout>

### The kernel matrix

The dot products between the images of the training patterns are arranged in an $N \times N$ matrix, the <T id="matrice-kernel">**kernel matrix**</T> (or Gram matrix):

$$
K = \{k(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j)\}_{i,j=1}^N,
$$

symmetric because the kernel is symmetric.

**Does every kernel function compute a dot product in some space?** No: the property holds only for kernels that produce **positive semidefinite** kernel matrices (<T id="mercer">**Mercer’s theorem**</T>), that is, with non-negative eigenvalues.

Closure properties: if $k_1$ and $k_2$ are kernels, so are $k_1 + k_2$, $\alpha k_1$ (with $\alpha > 0$) and $k_1 k_2$.

### SVM with kernels

The primal in the feature space is the same as the soft margin one with $\Phi(\mathbf{x}_i)$ in place of $\mathbf{x}_i$ (but $\mathbf{w}$ lives in the feature space, which can make the problem intractable). The **dual**, instead, is tractable:

<Callout type="definition" title="Dual problem in the feature space">

Find $\{\alpha_i\}$ that maximize

$$
Q(\boldsymbol{\alpha}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_i - \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\alpha_i\alpha_j d_i d_j\,\underbrace{k(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j)}_{K_{ij}}
$$

subject to $\sum_i\alpha_i d_i = 0$ and $0 \le \alpha_i \le C$.

</Callout>

The kernel embeds the mapping to the feature space. The dependence on $N$ (rather than on the dimension of the space) now becomes an **advantage**, because the feature space can have a very high or infinite dimension.

<Callout type="abstract" title="Summary of the procedure">

1. We have the training set $T = \{(\mathbf{x}_i, d_i)\}$.
2. We choose the parameter $C$ and the kernel function $k$.
3. We compute the kernel matrix $K$.
4. We solve the optimization problem (quadratic programming), finding $\{\alpha_i\}$.
5. We compute the bias $b$ from the multipliers and the kernel matrix.
6. For a new pattern $\mathbf{x}$: $\mathbf{w}^T\Phi(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_i\alpha_i d_i\,k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}_i) + b$ (without ever computing $\mathbf{w}$!), and we classify with the sign.

**Sparsity**: the solution is sparse in the $\alpha$, because all the multipliers of the non-support vectors are zero.

</Callout>

At test time:

<Formula f={F.kernelDecision} />

The $\mathbf{x}_i$ (the support vectors) are stored for the test phase: does it resemble K-NN?

### “Architectural” view of an SVM

<Figure n="13.7" title="The architecture of an SVM" size="wide">
  <Architecture />
  <Caption>“Architecture” of an SVM: the input layer $\mathbf{x}$ is connected to a hidden layer of units that compute the kernel $K(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}_i)$ with the support vectors; the outputs, weighted by $\alpha_i d_i$, are summed with the bias $b$ in the output neuron. Number of hidden units = number of support vectors. Here an SVM with RBF kernel trained on 10 points: drag the pattern $\mathbf{x}$ and change $\sigma$.</Caption>
</Figure>

The number of “hidden units” is equal to the **number of support vectors**, but they are not exactly the same units as in a neural network: the hidden units of a network have **adaptive free parameters**, whereas here the “centers” are the support vectors themselves and the function is fixed by the kernel. It is only an illustrative view: to be compared with neural networks (an adaptive form of LBE) in terms of complexity control, efficiency and other characteristics.

### Examples of kernels

- **Polynomial**: $k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}_i) = (\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{x}_i + 1)^p$, with $p$ chosen by the user.
- **RBF** (*Radial Basis Function*, also called **Gaussian kernel**): $k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}_i) = e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_i\|^2}$, with $\sigma^2$ chosen by the user. With very “narrow” kernels (small $\sigma$) the response on $\mathbf{x}_i$ is just $d_i$ (it resembles 1-NN!).
- **Two-layer perceptron**: $k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}_i) = \tanh(\beta_0\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{x}_i + \beta_1)$, with $\beta_0 > 0$ and $\beta_1 < 0$.

<Callout type="warning" title="Important">

For the polynomial kernel and the RBF we **always** obtain a valid kernel (a dot product), whereas for the two-layer perceptron Mercer’s theorem holds only for some choices of $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$. An RBF kernel always corresponds to a feature space of **infinite dimension**.

</Callout>

## Part II — SVM for nonlinear regression

### The problem

We have $d = f(\mathbf{x}) + v$, with $f$ unknown and noise $v$ independent of $\mathbf{x}$. We estimate $d$ with a linear expansion of nonlinear functions:

$$
y = h(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{w}^T\Phi(\mathbf{x}), \qquad \mathbf{w} = (b, w_1, \dots, w_{m_1})^T, \quad \Phi(\mathbf{x}) = (1, \phi_1(\mathbf{x}), \dots, \phi_{m_1}(\mathbf{x}))^T.
$$

### The $\varepsilon$-insensitive loss

<Formula f={F.epsLoss} />

<Figure n="13.8" title="The ε-insensitive loss" size="wide">
  <EpsLoss />
  <Caption>The $\varepsilon$-insensitive loss (or *soft margin loss*) as a function of $d - y$: it is zero between $-\varepsilon$ and $+\varepsilon$ and grows linearly outside. Drag the residual and change $\varepsilon$.</Caption>
</Figure>

Errors smaller than $\varepsilon$ **cost nothing**: a **tube** of width $\varepsilon$ forms around the prediction, and only the points outside the tube contribute to the loss. Once again, **only the support vectors drive the solution**: in classification they were the points beyond the margin, here they are the points outside the tube.

### Formulation

We introduce two sets of non-negative slack variables $\xi_i$ and $\xi'_i$ (above and below the tube):

$$
-\xi'_i - \varepsilon \le d_i - \mathbf{w}^T\Phi(\mathbf{x}_i) \le \varepsilon + \xi_i.
$$

<Callout type="definition" title="Primal problem — SVM for regression">

Minimize

$$
\Psi(\mathbf{w}, \boldsymbol{\xi}, \boldsymbol{\xi}') = \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{w} + C\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\xi_i + \xi'_i)
$$

subject to $d_i - \mathbf{w}^T\Phi(\mathbf{x}_i) \le \varepsilon + \xi_i$, $\;\mathbf{w}^T\Phi(\mathbf{x}_i) - d_i \le \varepsilon + \xi'_i$, $\;\xi_i \ge 0$, $\;\xi'_i \ge 0$.

</Callout>

<Callout type="definition" title="Dual problem — SVM for regression*">

Find $\{\alpha_i\}$ and $\{\alpha'_i\}$ that maximize

$$
Q(\boldsymbol{\alpha}, \boldsymbol{\alpha}') = \sum_{i=1}^{N}d_i(\alpha_i - \alpha'_i) - \varepsilon\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\alpha_i + \alpha'_i) - \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}(\alpha_i - \alpha'_i)(\alpha_j - \alpha'_j)\,k(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j)
$$

subject to $\sum_i(\alpha_i - \alpha'_i) = 0$ and $0 \le \alpha_i, \alpha'_i \le C$.

</Callout>

From the solution:

$$
\mathbf{w} = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\underbrace{(\alpha_i - \alpha'_i)}_{\gamma_i}\Phi(\mathbf{x}_i), \qquad h(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\gamma_i\,\Phi^T(\mathbf{x}_i)\Phi(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\gamma_i\,k(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}).
$$

The **support vectors** correspond to the nonzero $\gamma_i$.

<Callout type="abstract" title="Summary for regression">

1. Choose the parameters $C$ and $\varepsilon$.
2. Choose a kernel $k$.
3. Compute the kernel matrix $K$.
4. Solve the dual and obtain the $\gamma_i$.
5. Compute the optimal bias.
6. The estimated function is a linear combination of dot products in a feature space that we can ignore: $h(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_i\gamma_i\,k(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x})$.

</Callout>
